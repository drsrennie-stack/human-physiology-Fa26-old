Mission 2, Build the Molecular Toolkit

Instructor lecture narrative. Silverthorn chapter 2. Written to teach from, in eight recording segments. Dr. Sharilyn Rennie.

How to use this

Same format as Mission 1. This is the lecture, not an outline. The slides carry the content you point at, this carries the argument, and each concept is one recording segment.

The problem this mission solves

Last week ended with five components: sensor, afferent pathway, integrating center, efferent pathway, effector. Every one of them is a protein doing something, and the course has not yet said how a protein does anything at all. That is the gap this week fills.

The framing to use throughout: you are not learning chemistry, you are collecting the small number of things molecules have to be able to do before any of last week's pathway is physically possible. Detect. Change shape. Speed something up. Pay for it.

What this covers, and what it deliberately does not

Chapter 2, restricted to what physiology needs. Water and the hydrophobic effect, pH and buffering, protein structure and binding, enzymes and their regulation, and ATP as the coupling currency.

Not here: nucleic acid structure and the detail of transcription and translation. Students meet gene expression where it does work, in the steroid hormone material in the endocrine week. Teaching it now is a chapter of biochemistry they will have forgotten by the time it matters. This is a deliberate deprioritization, not an omission, and it is worth saying so out loud in class.

Course competencies this lecture serves

Number 2 on the Yuba syllabus, describing and distinguishing the roles of the major classes of biomolecules in living cells, and it feeds numbers 4 and 6 through the enzyme assay in lab.

Opening segment

The mission

About 6 minutes.

Open by going back to the pathway diagram from last week and pointing at each box in turn. A sensor. What is a sensor, physically? A protein that changes shape when something binds to it. An integrating center that compares an input against a setpoint. What is doing the comparing? Proteins again. An effector that produces a response. Muscle protein, or an enzyme changing a reaction rate, or a transport protein moving something across a membrane.

So the whole of last week rests on molecules, and we have said nothing about how any of them work.

Here is the frame for the week. You are not taking a chemistry course this week. You are collecting the short list of things a molecule has to be able to do before last week's diagram can exist in a real body. There are four. It has to be able to sit in the right place. It has to detect something and change shape. It has to make a reaction go fast enough to matter. And something has to pay for all of it.

Tell them the payoff up front. By Friday they will be able to look at any component of a control pathway and say what kind of molecule is doing that job and what property of it makes the job possible.

Transition into concept 1

Start with the one that decides everything else, because the molecule that shapes the entire toolkit is not a protein at all.

Concept 1 of 8

Water, and why things dissolve or do not

About 13 minutes.

One fact, and a surprising amount of physiology falls out of it.

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons in a water molecule harder than hydrogen does. That leaves the oxygen end slightly negative and the hydrogen ends slightly positive. A molecule with that kind of charge separation is polar.

Because of it, water molecules attract each other. Those attractions are hydrogen bonds. Any one of them is weak, far weaker than a covalent bond, and there are so many that collectively they run the show.

What that does to everything else

Polar and charged substances dissolve in water because water molecules can arrange themselves around them and interact favorably. Those substances are hydrophilic. Nonpolar substances cannot participate, so they are hydrophobic.

Now be careful, because this is where students learn it wrong and keep it wrong for two years. Nonpolar molecules in water clump together. The reason is not that they are attracted to each other. The reason is that water is far more comfortable hydrogen bonding with itself, and it excludes them. Pushing the nonpolar molecules together minimizes how much water has to be disturbed. So the clumping is driven by what water gains, not by what the oil molecules want.

Misconception to head off

Ask them why oil forms droplets in water. Almost everyone answers that oil is attracted to oil. Correct it directly. The oil is what is left over after water has organized itself. This matters in three weeks, because it is the entire reason a lipid bilayer forms without anything assembling it.

Where you will use this

Say all three now, briefly, because it makes the fact feel worth learning.

  • The membrane in week 3 forms because phospholipids have a water loving head and a water fearing tail, and water does the assembling.
  • How a hormone travels in the blood depends on this. Water soluble hormones dissolve in plasma and travel freely. Lipid soluble ones do not, and need a carrier protein.
  • Where a receptor sits, which they met last week, follows from it. If a signal cannot cross a lipid membrane, its receptor has to be on the surface.

Where to stop and ask

Ask them to predict where a steroid hormone's receptor is, given that steroids are lipid soluble. They can get this from last week plus the last two minutes, and getting it right is the moment the chemistry starts to feel like physiology.

Transition into concept 2

Water does one more thing that matters more than any of that. It comes apart, slightly, and the amount it comes apart is regulated to a tolerance tighter than almost anything else in the body.

Concept 2 of 8

pH and buffering

About 15 minutes.

A small fraction of water molecules dissociate into hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions. pH is a measure of the hydrogen ion concentration, and it runs on a negative logarithmic scale, so a lower pH means more hydrogen ions and a change of one pH unit is a tenfold change in concentration.

Make the logarithm concrete rather than leaving it as a definition. A pH of 7.4 and a pH of 7.1 do not differ by a small amount. They differ by a factor of two in hydrogen ion concentration.

The range the body defends

Arterial pH is held between about 7.35 and 7.45. That is the tightest regulated variable you will meet all semester, and it is worth pausing on how narrow it is relative to what the body tolerates elsewhere. Sodium has a range of about ten millimoles per liter. Arterial pH has a window a tenth of a unit wide on either side.

The reason it matters so much is protein shape, which is the next concept. Hydrogen ions interact with charged groups on proteins, and changing the hydrogen ion concentration changes how those groups behave, which changes protein shape, which changes function. Every enzyme, every receptor and every channel in the body is affected.

Buffers

A buffer is a substance that moderates a change in pH by accepting hydrogen ions when there are too many and releasing them when there are too few.

The main extracellular buffer is the bicarbonate system:

CO2 + H2O ⇄ H2CO3 ⇄ H+ + HCO3−

Read the equation in both directions out loud, because that is where the physiology is. Add hydrogen ions and the reaction is pushed left, consuming them and producing carbon dioxide, which you then breathe off. Remove hydrogen ions and it runs right, generating more.

Then say the thing that makes this system special, and flag it as a promise you are keeping in week 13. Both ends of this equation are under physiological control. The lungs control carbon dioxide, minute to minute. The kidneys control bicarbonate, over hours to days. That is why acid base balance gets two separate weeks in this course, one fast lever and one slow one.

pH = 6.1 + log ( [HCO3−] / (0.03 × PCO2) )

6.1, the pK for this system in plasma. [HCO3−], plasma bicarbonate in mmol/L, the kidney's term. PCO2, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in mmHg, the lung's term. 0.03, the solubility coefficient that converts a pressure into a concentration.

Do not make them calculate with it this week. Make them read it. The numerator is renal, the denominator is respiratory, and pH is the ratio between them. That single reading is worth more than any arithmetic, and when a patient's blood gas comes up in week 13 they will already know which organ owns which term.

Predict, then check

Ask what happens to pH if a patient stops ventilating adequately. Let them work it. Carbon dioxide rises, the denominator gets bigger, the ratio falls, pH falls. Then ask what the kidney could do about it, and they will see that raising bicarbonate restores the ratio without touching the carbon dioxide. That is compensation, and they just derived it.

Transition into concept 3

The reason pH is defended that tightly is the subject of the next segment.

Concept 3 of 8

Protein structure

About 13 minutes.

Proteins are chains of amino acids, and the whole of their behavior comes from the fact that the chain folds into a specific three dimensional shape.

Four levels, and go through them quickly, because the point is not the vocabulary.

  • Primary structure. The sequence of amino acids, set by the gene.
  • Secondary structure. Local patterns, the alpha helix and the beta sheet, held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
  • Tertiary structure. The whole chain folded into its working shape, held largely by interactions among the amino acid side chains, including the hydrophobic effect from concept 1 burying nonpolar side chains away from water.
  • Quaternary structure. Two or more folded chains assembled together. Hemoglobin is four.

The sentence that carries the rest of the course: shape is function. A protein does what it does because of the shape it holds, and anything that changes the shape changes what it can do. Every receptor, every channel, every enzyme and every transporter you meet this semester is a shape with a site on it.

Denaturation

Denaturation is loss of that three dimensional shape, with the sequence still intact. The chain is unchanged and the function is gone.

The things that cause it are the things the body regulates, which is not a coincidence. Temperature. pH. Salt concentration. Now the tight pH range from concept 2 has a reason attached to it, and so does the temperature setpoint from last week.

Give them the clinical version. A fever of 40 C (104 F) is uncomfortable. A core temperature of 43 C (109.4 F) is lethal, and it is lethal because proteins begin to denature at a rate the body cannot repair. The setpoint is not defended out of preference.

Draw this

A folded chain with a notch in it, then the same chain unfolded with no notch. Label the notch as the binding site. That single before and after is what students should picture every time you say denaturation for the rest of the year, and it takes fifteen seconds to draw.

Transition into concept 4

You have drawn a notch. The next segment is entirely about what happens when something fits into it.

Concept 4 of 8

Binding, and what makes it specific

About 14 minutes. This is the segment that connects the whole week back to last week.

A protein has a binding site, a region whose shape and charge distribution accommodate a particular molecule. The molecule that binds is the ligand.

Four properties, and every one of them turns up again in the endocrine week and again in pharmacology.

Specificity

How selective the binding site is about what it will accept. High specificity means one ligand and almost nothing else. Lower specificity means a family of related molecules can bind, which is exactly why a drug intended for one receptor produces effects at another.

Affinity

How tightly the protein holds the ligand once bound. Two proteins can bind the same ligand with very different affinities, and the one with higher affinity will take it from the other.

Saturation

A protein has a finite number of binding sites. As ligand concentration rises, more sites fill, until all of them are occupied and the response cannot increase no matter how much more ligand arrives. Draw the curve: rising, then bending, then flat.

This is the shape of nearly every dose response and transport relationship in the course. Say that now so they recognize it when it reappears in week 3 with carrier mediated transport and in week 13 with hemoglobin.

Competition

Two molecules that both fit a site compete for it, and the winner depends on relative concentration and relative affinity. That is how a competitive inhibitor works, and it is why carbon monoxide is dangerous at concentrations that sound trivial: it binds the same site on hemoglobin that oxygen does, with far higher affinity.

Where to stop and ask

Send them back to last week's receptor material. An antagonist binds a receptor and produces no response. Which of these four properties is doing the work? Specificity gets it into the site. Competition is why it blocks the natural signal. Affinity is why some antagonists are harder to displace than others. That is three of the four in one clinical example, and they built it themselves.

Transition into concept 5

Binding on its own accomplishes nothing. The next segment is about proteins that bind something and then do something to it.

Concept 5 of 8

Enzymes and reaction rate

About 14 minutes.

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed by it. The molecule it acts on is the substrate, and it binds at the active site, which is a binding site with catalytic machinery in it.

What an enzyme actually changes, and what it does not

Be precise here, because the sloppy version causes trouble later.

A reaction needs an input of energy to get started, the activation energy. An enzyme lowers that barrier, so a far larger fraction of collisions are productive and the reaction proceeds at a useful rate.

What an enzyme does not do is change the equilibrium position or make an energetically unfavorable reaction favorable. It changes how fast equilibrium is reached, not where equilibrium is.

Misconception to head off

Students routinely leave this thinking enzymes make reactions happen that otherwise could not. They do not. The reaction was always thermodynamically possible and it was too slow to be useful. If it needs to be pushed uphill, that costs energy, and that is concept 7.

Rate, and the shape of the curve

Reaction rate rises as substrate concentration rises, and then it flattens, because the enzyme molecules become saturated. Every active site is occupied, and adding more substrate cannot help. That is the same saturation curve from concept 4, and it is worth naming the repetition out loud.

Two terms to attach to the curve, at the level they need this week. Vmax is the maximum rate, reached when the enzyme is saturated. Km is the substrate concentration that produces half of Vmax, and it is a practical measure of affinity: a lower Km means the enzyme reaches half speed at a lower substrate concentration, so it binds more avidly.

The physiological habit this builds, and you will keep coming back to it: almost nothing in the body is about how much of something there is. It is about how fast it is being made and how fast it is being removed. A concentration that sits still is two rates that happen to match. That is the same idea as last week's steady state, arrived at from the molecular side.

Draw this

One set of axes, rate on the vertical and substrate concentration on the horizontal. Draw the curve rising and flattening. Mark Vmax as the plateau and Km on the horizontal axis at half that height. Leave it on the board for concept 6, because you are about to change it twice.

Transition into concept 6

An enzyme that always runs flat out is not a control system. What makes it useful physiologically is that its rate can be changed.

Concept 6 of 8

Enzyme regulation

About 13 minutes.

This is the segment that turns chemistry into physiology, so say why at the start. Last week you told them a control pathway adjusts a response. At the molecular level, adjusting a response usually means changing an enzyme's rate. These are the mechanisms that do it.

Conditions

Temperature and pH change enzyme rate, because they change protein shape. Every enzyme has a range where it works best, and the body defends those conditions. That is now the third time this week that homeostasis has turned out to exist for the sake of protein shape.

Inhibitors

A competitive inhibitor binds the active site and competes with substrate. Add enough substrate and you can outcompete it, so Vmax is unchanged and it takes more substrate to reach half of it.

A noncompetitive inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the enzyme, so the active site works less well or not at all. More substrate does not rescue it, and Vmax falls.

Go back to the curve on the board and draw both, one at a time. The competitive curve reaches the same plateau further to the right. The noncompetitive curve plateaus lower. Those two pictures are worth more than the definitions.

Allosteric and covalent modulation

An allosteric modulator binds a site other than the active site and changes the enzyme's shape, which can either increase or decrease activity. Noncompetitive inhibition is the negative case of this.

Covalent modulation means a chemical group is attached to the enzyme, changing its activity. The one that matters is phosphorylation, adding a phosphate group. Kinases add phosphate, phosphatases remove it, and a very large fraction of the body's minute to minute control runs through that one switch.

Now connect it. Last week you told them a surface receptor relays its message inward through a cascade. That cascade is largely enzymes phosphorylating other enzymes. The amplification they were told about is this: one activated kinase can phosphorylate many targets, and each of those can act on many more.

Where to stop and ask

Ask what advantage phosphorylation has over synthesizing a new enzyme when a cell needs to change a rate. Answer: speed and reversibility. Making a protein takes minutes to hours. Adding a phosphate takes seconds and a phosphatase can undo it. That is why fast control is covalent and slow control is genomic, which is the same fast-and-slow split they met last week with the nervous and endocrine systems.

Transition into concept 7

Everything so far has been about rate. Nothing so far has paid for anything.

Concept 7 of 8

ATP and energy coupling

About 12 minutes.

Some reactions release energy and proceed on their own. Others require an input of energy and will not proceed without one. The body is full of the second kind, because building things and moving things against a gradient both cost energy.

ATP is how the cell pays. Breaking the bond to its terminal phosphate group releases energy, producing ADP and inorganic phosphate.

ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi + energy

The important word is coupling. The energy is not released into the room. The energy releasing reaction is coupled to the energy requiring one, usually by transferring the phosphate group directly onto the protein doing the work, which changes its shape and makes it do something. Phosphorylation again, in a different role.

The three things it pays for

Name all three now, because they meet all three within six weeks.

  • Transport. Moving a substance against its concentration gradient. The sodium potassium pump in week 3 is the example, and it consumes a substantial fraction of resting energy use.
  • Movement. Muscle contraction in week 7, where ATP binding and hydrolysis drive the cycle.
  • Synthesis. Building molecules the cell needs, including the proteins in every other concept this week.

Bring it back to last week explicitly. You told them homeostasis costs energy and that a steady value is being actively held. This is the currency that holds it. When a cell runs out of ATP, transport stops, gradients dissipate, and the steady state collapses toward equilibrium. Equilibrium was the word you used for dead. Now they can see the mechanism behind that sentence.

Transition into concept 8

Time to put the toolkit against the diagram it was built for.

Concept 8 of 8

The toolkit against the pathway

About 12 minutes.

Put last week's five component diagram back on the screen and go through it one box at a time, asking what molecule does that job and which property makes it possible. Let them answer. They can do all of it now.

The closing problem

Give them a patient who is profoundly hypothermic, with a core temperature of 30 C (86 F), and ask what happens to the control pathways from last week. Work it with them rather than telling them.

Enzyme rates fall, because temperature is one of the conditions in concept 6. ATP production falls with them. Transport that costs ATP slows, so gradients start to dissipate. Sensors and effectors are proteins whose shape is temperature dependent, so detection and response both degrade. The pathway is not broken at any one component. Every component is running slow at once.

Then ask the question that makes it physiology rather than trivia: which of last week's five failure modes is this? It is none of them, and that is the point. It is the sixth situation, the one where the components work and the system as a whole cannot meet the demand.

What they carry into next week

  • Water is polar and hydrogen bonds, and that decides what dissolves, how signals travel and where receptors sit.
  • pH is defended tightly because hydrogen ions change protein shape, and the bicarbonate system has one lung term and one kidney term.
  • Shape is function. Denaturation destroys function without touching the sequence.
  • Binding has specificity, affinity, saturation and competition, and those four explain most of pharmacology.
  • Enzymes change how fast equilibrium is reached, never where it is, and their rate is what physiological control adjusts.
  • ATP coupling pays for transport, movement and synthesis, which is what holding a steady state actually costs.

Then set up week 3. They now have molecules that can detect, change shape, catalyze and be paid for. What they do not have is a container. Next week is the membrane, and it builds itself out of concept 1.
