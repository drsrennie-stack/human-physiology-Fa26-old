What I am aiming for

The rules are the floor, not the goal.

The legal standard this course has to meet is WCAG 2.2 Level AA, along with Section 508 and the accessibility requirements that come with teaching online in the California Community Colleges. Every page here is built to clear that. Where a page can reach Level AAA without making the physiology worse, it does, and further down you can see exactly which parts of AAA it reaches and why.

The reason is simple. In a class you attend in person, a page that does not work is an annoyance you can get around by asking the person next to you. Online, it is the whole course. You should be spending your effort on physiology, not on fighting the website.

Something else worth saying out loud. You do not need a diagnosis, a letter, or a reason to ask me to fix something. If a page is hard to read, if a color is hard to see, if the text is too small on your phone, if a tool does not work with the software you use, tell me in the Virtual Office. It is a build problem and I will fix it. Formal accommodations through DSPS are a separate and also good route, and the syllabus explains how to start that, but you do not have to wait for it to ask me for a change.

How it was built

Most of what makes this course accessible was decided for teaching reasons first. The two turned out to be the same thing.

These are the design decisions behind the course, and the accessibility criteria they happen to satisfy. I am listing them together because I think it is the honest account: I did not bolt accessibility on at the end, and I also did not set out to chase criteria. I built the course the way I think physiology should be taught, and this is where that landed.

Some things that are not in any standard

  • The drawing work is done by hand, on paper. Nobody has to own a stylus or a tablet to do the part of this course that carries the most weight.
  • Every tool has a QR route, so you can reach it from a phone without typing an address.
  • The pages print properly, so you can work from paper if that is how you study.
  • The recall cards, the study guide, the syllabus, the schedule and the lab manual are never locked. Only the week teaching pages open on a date, and that is because they are being written, not to hold anything back from you.
  • Every week unlocks the Saturday evening before it opens, so if your week runs on a different rhythm than mine you are not stuck waiting.

Keyboard and focus

Every interactive element on the shared navigation is reachable by keyboard in the order it appears, and focus is shown as a 3 pixel gold outline that is visible against every background used in the course. Movement and animation are switched off for anyone whose device asks for reduced motion. The navigation is hidden when a page is printed.

What the checks found, and what I did about it

An accessibility page that only lists successes is not worth much, so here is what the pass on September 5, 2026 actually turned up and how each one was fixed.

  • Thirteen headings and labels sat at 4.36:1 across nine pages, which is just under the 4.5:1 AA floor for small bold text. The gray was darkened to 7.05:1, which clears AAA.
  • A diagram in the pulmonary function lab was marked as an image but contained things you could tab to. A screen reader would have announced it as a picture and then hidden the eight interactive brackets inside it. It is now marked as a group, which is what it is.
  • The course tools button was sitting on top of the pen toolbar on the slide decks, covering part of the color swatches. It failed the WCAG 2.2 rule that a control has to be at least 24 by 24 pixels and not covered. The toolbar now sits above it.
  • Three pages had no main content landmark and one had no heading. Both are fixed, so all 98 pages now announce their structure properly.

What your own computer already does

Most of this is already installed on the device in front of you, and almost nobody is ever told it is there.

You do not need to buy anything or install anything to have a page read to you, made bigger, or stripped down to plain text. If any of this would help you, use it. It is not a special arrangement and you do not have to tell anyone you are using it.

On a Mac

  • VoiceOver reads the whole screen. Press Command and F5 together. Press it again to turn it off. If your Mac has Touch ID, hold Command and press Touch ID three times quickly.
  • Speak Selection reads just the part you highlight. Turn it on in System Settings, Accessibility, Spoken Content, then select some text and press the key you set.
  • Reader in Safari strips a page down to the text at whatever size you like. Look for the icon at the left of the address bar.

On Windows

  • Narrator reads the whole screen. Press the Windows key, Control and Enter together. The same keys turn it off.
  • Immersive Reader in Microsoft Edge strips a page to plain text, changes the spacing, and reads it aloud. Open a page and look for the book icon in the address bar, or press F9.
  • NVDA is a free screen reader many people prefer to Narrator. It is a download from nvaccess.org and it costs nothing.

On a phone or tablet

  • iPhone and iPad. Settings, Accessibility, Spoken Content, then switch on Speak Screen. After that, swipe down from the top with two fingers and the page is read to you. VoiceOver is in the same Accessibility menu.
  • Android. Settings, Accessibility, then TalkBack for the full screen reader or Select to Speak to have just the part you choose read out.

In any browser

  • Hold Command and press plus on a Mac, or Control and plus on Windows, to make everything bigger. It works on every page in this course and nothing breaks.
  • Reader View, in Safari, Firefox and Edge, gives you the text on its own with no layout.

If you use a screen reader and something on a page of mine gets in your way, that is a fault on my side. Tell me which page and what happened.

The Listen button, and what it is not

There is a Listen to this page button at the bottom of every page. It is worth being precise about what it does.

It is text to speech. Press it and your browser reads the page out loud, one paragraph at a time, highlighting each one as it goes so you can follow with your eyes and your ears together. A small control appears in the corner with pause and a speed setting, so you can slow it down or run it faster than you read.

It is not a screen reader, and it is not a substitute for one. A screen reader does far more: it describes buttons and images, tells you what state things are in, and lets you move around by heading or by link. If you use one, keep using it. Yours is better than this and it is already set up the way you like it.

The Listen button is there for a different reason. Some weeks the reading is long and your eyes are tired. Some of you study in the car before a shift. Some of you take material in better through your ears than off a page, and some of you are reading physiology in your second language. Any of those is a good enough reason to press it.

What is not done yet

Named here rather than left for you to discover.

  • Screen reader testing is not yet documented. The pages are built to the structural rules a screen reader depends on, and the automated checks confirm the structure, but a recorded pass with NVDA and VoiceOver has not been done and written up. That is the next piece of verification work.
  • The twenty Week 1 concept videos do not have captions yet. Every one of them has a written notes document beside it covering the same material, so the content is available in text today, but captions are required and they are being added.
  • Transcripts are not posted for the videos. Same position as captions.
  • The Word and PDF downloads are not tagged. The lab manual and the printable forms read correctly on screen and in print, but their internal structure is not marked up for assistive technology yet. The HTML version of everything is, so there is an accessible route to all of it in the meantime.
  • The Canvas side of the course is audited separately. This statement covers the course site. Canvas pages, quizzes and discussions are checked against the same standard, and anything found there is fixed there.
  • One study tool still carries branding from my anatomy course. Cosmetic rather than a barrier, and on the list.

The standards this is measured against

For anyone reviewing the course rather than taking it.

  • WCAG 2.2 Level AA is the floor for every page. Level AAA is reached where it can be, and the criteria met are named in the construction section above.
  • Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act, which is the basis of Section D of the CVC-OEI Course Design Rubric, sixteen elements scored as either aligned or incomplete.
  • ADA Title II. The Department of Justice rule requires public institutions to meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA. The compliance date for larger public entities is April 26, 2027, and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones. This course is built to WCAG 2.2 AA, which is the later standard, so it is ahead of that requirement rather than working toward it.

Evidence on file: 26 per-page compliance notes with measured contrast pairs and written keyboard flows, and the automated report summarized above, re-run whenever the site changes.
