What this week covers

Sensing the world, and the responses you do not control. Work through these in order. Each line is one thing you will be able to do by Sunday.

  1. Sensory transduction. Explain how a stimulus is converted into a receptor potential and how that graded potential becomes a train of action potentials in the sensory neuron.
  2. Receptor classification. Classify a receptor by stimulus type as a mechanoreceptor, chemoreceptor, thermoreceptor, photoreceptor, or nociceptor and give a body location for each.
  3. Stimulus coding. Explain how the nervous system encodes modality, location, intensity, and duration and identify the labeled line principle.
  4. Receptive fields and acuity. Relate receptive field size and lateral inhibition to two point discrimination and predict which body regions have the finest acuity.
  5. Receptor adaptation. Distinguish tonic from phasic receptors by their adaptation rate and match each to a stimulus the body needs to monitor continuously or only at onset.
  6. Somatosensory pathways. Trace touch and pain information from receptor to cortex and explain why the somatosensory homunculus is disproportionate.
  7. Pain and its modulation. Distinguish fast and slow pain by fiber type, explain referred pain by convergence, and describe how gate control and endogenous opioids reduce pain transmission.
  8. Tactile mapping and adaptation testing. Measure two point discrimination at several body sites and show receptor adaptation and referred sensation experimentally.
  9. Optics and accommodation. Explain refraction by the cornea and lens and describe how accommodation and the pupillary reflex focus near and far images.
  10. Phototransduction. Trace phototransduction from photon absorption by retinal through the change in cyclic GMP to the decrease in glutamate release and explain why photoreceptors hyperpolarize to light.
  11. Rods cones and visual processing. Compare rods and cones by sensitivity and acuity and trace the visual pathway to predict the field deficit produced by a lesion at the optic nerve or the optic chiasm.
  12. Refractive errors and clinical vision. Explain myopia, hyperopia, presbyopia, and astigmatism in terms of focal point and eyeball or lens shape and state the corrective lens for each.
  13. Auditory transduction. Trace sound from the tympanic membrane through the ossicles and cochlear fluid to hair cell bending and auditory nerve firing and explain how pitch and loudness are coded.
  14. Conductive and sensorineural hearing loss. Distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss by the site of the lesion and by the expected Weber and Rinne results.
  15. Equilibrium. Explain how the semicircular canals detect angular acceleration and how the utricle and saccule detect linear acceleration and head position.
  16. Taste and smell. Compare gustatory and olfactory transduction and explain why olfactory input reaches the limbic system without a thalamic relay.
  17. Vision testing. Perform visual acuity, blind spot, accommodation, and color vision testing and interpret each result.
  18. Hearing and equilibrium testing. Perform Weber and Rinne tuning fork tests and a balance test and interpret the findings.
  19. Autonomic and somatic organization. Contrast the somatic motor system with the autonomic two neuron pathway by neuron count and by effector tissue.
  20. Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. Compare the two autonomic divisions by CNS origin and ganglion location and predict the effect of each on heart rate and on the gastrointestinal tract.
  21. Autonomic neurotransmitters and receptors. Match nicotinic, muscarinic, alpha, and beta receptors to their neurotransmitter, their location, and the response each produces.
  22. Dual innervation and tonic control. Explain dual innervation and autonomic tone and predict the effect of removing one division from a dually innervated organ.
  23. Autonomic pharmacology. Predict the effect of a beta blocker or a muscarinic antagonist on a named organ from the receptor it targets.
  24. Autonomic function testing. Measure heart rate variability and the response to a cold pressor or orthostatic challenge and interpret the result as sympathetic or parasympathetic dominance.
