How this course is built

Start

Your body has one job it can never stop doing: keep the conditions inside you steady enough that you stay alive.

  • Everything else your body does is in service of that one job. Every heartbeat, every breath, every meal you digest, every hormone released.
  • That job is far too big to learn in one piece. So this course breaks it into smaller jobs, one problem at a time.
  • Each is a problem your body has to solve. Each one starts with something the body needs to get done, shows you the machinery it uses, then changes the conditions to see whether that machinery can keep up.
  • You are not memorizing a problem. You are learning the physiology inside it. The problem is there so the physiology has a reason to exist.

The one big job

Keep the conditions inside the body steady while the world outside, and your own activity inside, keep changing them.

Everything in this course hangs off that sentence. If the body loses the ability to do it, nothing else it can do matters.

Unit 1, Keep the Human Alive

Week 1: Maintain Control

Problem

Every problem after this one assumes this one worked.

  • There are dozens of conditions inside you that have to stay inside a narrow band. Body temperature, blood sugar, how acidic your blood is, how much salt and water you are carrying, and many more.
  • Nothing tells your body to hold those steady. It does it on its own, every second, awake or asleep.
  • Let one of them slip far enough and you have somewhere between a few minutes and a few days, depending on which one it was.
  • Today we work out what a system has to contain before it can hold anything steady at all.

Silverthorn chapter 1, plus the homeostatic reflex pathways section of chapter 6. Course competencies 1, 3, 4 and 5.

Week 1

Keep the conditions inside the body within survivable limits, while conditions inside and outside keep changing without warning.

The point of the exercise

Potassium. At 6.8 mM the heart is at risk within minutes, while the low pH and the fast breathing are serious but slower. Sorting told you which values are abnormal. It did not tell you why, or which one to deal with first, or whether any of them are related.

05

Foundations

Physiology is the study of function

Mechanism

Definitions to hold

  • Physiology is the study of the normal functioning of a living organism and its parts, including the physical and chemical processes involved.
  • Anatomy is the study of structure. Physiology asks what that structure does and how.
  • Pathophysiology is what function looks like when a normal mechanism fails.
06

Foundations

Two ways to answer why

Mechanism
  • Teleological answers say what for. Red cells carry oxygen because tissues need it.
  • Mechanistic answers say how. Hemoglobin binds oxygen because the oxygen partial pressure is high in the lung and the binding is cooperative.
  • Physiology wants the mechanistic answer. The teleological one is a memory aid, not an explanation.

Grading consequence

A teleological answer earns partial credit at best, because it restates the observation. If your sentence would still be true with the mechanism removed, you have not answered the question.

Test yourself by deleting every because clause. If what is left still says something, you wrote a description, not an explanation.

Example

Why do you shiver when you are cold?

Says what for

Because your body needs to warm back up.

Says how

Because temperature sensors in your skin detect the drop, the hypothalamus compares that reading with its target, and it sends motor signals that make muscle contract rapidly and repeatedly. Heat is a by-product of that contraction.

Both sentences are true. Only the second one lets you predict anything. Cut the nerve from the skin and the first sentence is still true, but the shivering stops.

Foundations

Emergent properties

Mechanism

An emergent property belongs to the whole system. Take the system apart and the property is not in any of the pieces.

Read each row across: the property, what it is made of, and what one piece can manage alone
The propertyMade ofWhat one piece alone can doWhat the whole can do
Blood pressureMillions of heart muscle cells inside a closed loop of vesselsOne cell can shorten. That is all. A single cell has no pressure.Contract in a timed sequence against resistance, producing a pressure you can measure in an arm
Concentrating urineAbout a million nephrons arranged in loops with a shared blood supplyOne nephron can move salt and water across a wallBuild a salt gradient down the kidney, so water can be pulled back and the urine leaves concentrated
ConsciousnessBillions of neurons wired into networksOne neuron can fire or not fireSomething we can measure and still cannot fully explain, which is worth saying out loud

Say it plainly

The rule you would expect: add up what the parts do and you get what the system does.

What breaks it: arrangement. Heart cells in a dish will beat, and they still produce no blood pressure, because pressure needs them joined in a particular order, squeezing into a closed container. The property does not come from the parts. It comes from the parts plus how they are put together.

Why you care: the thing that fails in a patient is almost always a whole-system property, and the cause is almost always down at the level of a molecule or a cell. Connecting those two levels in writing is what your patient file asks for every week.

Nursing

You monitor system properties and you act on causes, so you carry both levels at once.

Example: You chart a blood pressure, but the fluid you hang is aimed at a mechanism, not at the number on the monitor.

Radiologic technology

An image is a system-level property too. What appears on it is the sum of every tissue the beam passed through, not any single structure.

Example: A white-out on a chest film is one appearance with several possible causes: fluid, collapse, or consolidation. The image shows the property. It does not by itself name the mechanism.

Medicine

The number that fails is a whole-system property. The cause is almost always molecular or cellular, one or two levels down.

Example: Blood pressure falls in sepsis. The pressure is the system property. The cause is signaling molecules relaxing vessel walls, which is a molecular event.

Respiratory therapy

Oxygen saturation is a whole-system property of gas exchange, so a normal value does not mean every part is working.

Example: A patient can hold a normal saturation while carbon dioxide climbs, because saturation and ventilation are not the same measurement.

09

The internal environment

Naming the compartments

Mechanism

What the words mean

  • External environment is everything outside the body, and that includes the inside of the gut and the airways.
  • Internal environment is the fluid outside your cells but inside you. Its proper name is extracellular fluid, or ECF.
  • Your cells never touch the outside world. They sit in ECF, and ECF is what the body works to hold steady.

How the water is split

  • Total body water is about 60 percent of body mass.
    • About two thirds of it is inside cells.
    • About one third is outside cells, and that third splits into interstitial fluid and plasma.
  • Plasma is the only part that travels quickly around the body, which is why plasma is what gets sampled when someone draws blood.

The line everyone trips on

Food sitting in your stomach is not inside you yet.

The gut is a tube that runs through you and opens to the outside at both ends. Nothing has entered your internal environment until it has crossed a layer of cells and reached the fluid around them. The same is true of the air in your lungs.

Nursing

Where the extra fluid is sitting is what changes the things you see when you assess the patient.

Example: A patient can be visibly swollen, with fluid sitting in the interstitial space, and still be low on volume inside the vessels.

Radiologic technology

Contrast goes into the blood first and then leaks out into the tissue. So when you scan decides which of those two you are actually looking at.

Example: An arterial-phase scan catches contrast still inside the vessels. A delayed scan catches it after it has moved out into the interstitial space. Same injection, two different-looking images, because the contrast moved compartments.

Medicine

Before you pick an IV fluid you have to decide which fluid space you are trying to fill.

Example: A liter of isotonic saline stays largely in the extracellular fluid. A liter of 5 percent dextrose in water spreads across total body water, so much less of it stays in the vessels.

Respiratory therapy

Mucus in the airway counts as outside the body, in the same way that food sitting in your gut is still outside you.

Example: A dry airway is a local humidity problem. It is not the same thing as whole-body dehydration, and it is not fixed the same way.

10

The central concept

Homeostasis is not equilibrium

Mechanism

This is the distinction students lose the most points on all term, so get it now.

  • Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment. Relatively is doing real work in that sentence.
  • Dynamic steady state is what it actually is: material and energy move continuously, but the amount in each compartment does not change much.

Worked

Extracellular sodium sits near 145 mM and intracellular near 12 mM. Sodium leaks in constantly down a steep gradient. The concentration holds because the sodium potassium pump spends ATP throwing it back out, every second, for your whole life. Stop the ATP and the gradient collapses within minutes. Nothing about that is equilibrium.

Nursing

When blood flow drops, cells can no longer hold their gradients. You see that in the patient before you see it in the labs.

Example: Cool mottled skin and a rising lactate are gradient failure becoming visible at the bedside.

Radiologic technology

A nuclear medicine tracer only builds up where cells are still alive and still spending energy. The picture is a map of which tissue is still working.

Example: Technetium sestamibi concentrates in heart muscle because living mitochondria maintain their charge gradient. Dead tissue cannot, so it takes up nothing. That is what makes the cold spot on the scan.

Medicine

Every gradient in the body costs energy to hold, and that bill never stops while the patient is alive.

Example: When perfusion stops, the sodium potassium pump stops with it, potassium leaks out of cells, and measured plasma potassium rises.

Respiratory therapy

Breathing is muscle work, and that work is the price of keeping carbon dioxide moving out.

Example: A patient who is tiring can no longer afford that gradient, which is why carbon dioxide starts to climb as they wear out.

Quantitative

Mass balance

Mechanism

If a substance is held at a constant amount, then what comes in must equal what goes out.

Total body load = intake + production − excretion − metabolism

Load is the total amount of the substance in the body, not its concentration.

What it says: a concentration can change for two entirely different reasons. The amount of substance changed, or the volume it is dissolved in changed. Never assume the first without ruling out the second.

Mass flow = concentration × volume flow

Units: mg/min = mg/mL × mL/min.

What it says: delivery is not concentration. A normal concentration with a collapsed flow still starves the tissue.

Work it

  • Plasma glucose is 90 mg/dL. Renal plasma flow is 600 mL/min. How much glucose reaches the kidney per minute?

Answer

90 mg/dL is 0.9 mg/mL. Mass flow = 0.9 mg/mL × 600 mL/min = 540 mg/min.

Now the physiology question: essentially none of it appears in the urine. Something is moving 540 mg back per minute against a gradient. Hold that thought for Week 14.

Nursing

Intake and output charting is just what went in minus what came out. Record it loosely and the problem hides in the gap.

Example: A patient in positive balance across three shifts has gained fluid, and the daily weight usually shows it before anything else does.

Radiologic technology

Contrast follows the same rule as everything else. What you put in stays in the patient until the kidney clears it out.

Example: A patient who had contrast yesterday has not finished clearing it today. A repeat study too soon adds to a load that is still there, which is why prior studies get checked before the next injection.

Medicine

A number can rise because there is more of the substance, or because there is less water. Those two need opposite treatments.

Example: A rising sodium can mean sodium was gained or water was lost. Treating the wrong one moves the patient in the wrong direction.

Respiratory therapy

Extra fluid ends up in the lung, and water in the lung gets in the way of gas crossing over.

Example: A patient several liters positive becomes harder to oxygenate, and the chest film tends to follow the balance sheet.

13

Quantitative

Clearance

Mechanism

Clearance = rate of removal ÷ plasma concentration

Units: mL/min. Clearance is a volume per time, not an amount per time.

What it says: clearance is the volume of plasma completely cleared of a substance per minute. No real volume is ever fully cleared. It is a way of expressing removal that does not change when the concentration changes.

  • Organs that clear things: kidney, liver, lung, skin, gut.
  • Clearance is how drug dosing intervals are set, and it is the reason kidney failure changes every dose a patient takes.

Predict before you look

A patient's kidney clearance of a drug halves. The dose stays the same and the dosing interval stays the same. What happens to the plasma concentration of the drug over the next several doses, and why?

Nursing

You are often the one who notices accumulation before a level comes back.

Example: Rising drowsiness in a patient whose kidney function dropped is a clearance problem until something else explains it.

Radiologic technology

Iodinated contrast is cleared almost entirely by the kidneys, which is why kidney function is the screening question before you inject.

Example: An eGFR or creatinine is checked ahead of a contrast study because a patient who cannot clear the dose holds it far longer than the protocol assumes. It is the same reason metformin gets held afterward.

Medicine

Clearance sets the dosing interval, so a failing organ changes every drug on the list at once.

Example: When kidney clearance halves and the interval does not change, the drug accumulates dose after dose.

Respiratory therapy

Sedatives cleared by the liver or kidney behave differently when those organs are struggling.

Example: The same sedation dose lasts far longer, and it shows up as a failed weaning trial rather than as an abnormal lab.

14

Control

Local control and reflex control

Mechanism

Nursing

Knowing whether the response is happening in one spot or across the whole body tells you what to expect when you do something about it.

Example: Warming one limb changes blood flow in that limb without changing the patient's core temperature.

Radiologic technology

Contrast only lights up tissue that is actually getting blood. So the bright areas are a picture of which local vessels are open.

Example: A tumor lights up because its own vessels are dilated and leaky, a purely local change. Nothing whole-body happened. The bright spot is local blood flow made visible.

Medicine

Local control does not need nerves or hormones, so it keeps working even when the long distance systems have failed.

Example: In a transplanted heart the nerves are cut, and local metabolic control of the coronary vessels still works normally.

Respiratory therapy

The lung sends blood to the parts that are getting air, and it does that locally, with no instruction from the brain.

Example: Low oxygen in one region narrows the vessels there, pushing blood toward regions that are actually being ventilated.

15

Control

The seven steps of a reflex

Response
  • Sensor or receptor: detects the change in the regulated variable.
  • Afferent path: carries the signal toward the integrating center. Afferent means arriving.
  • Integrating center: compares the signal with the set point and decides on a response.
  • Efferent path: carries the command out. Efferent means exiting.
  • Effector: the muscle or gland that does the physical work.

Diagnostic use

Break the sensor and the body never knows. Break the afferent path and the signal never arrives. Break the integrating center and the comparison is wrong, which is what fever is. Break the efferent path or the effector and the command is issued but nothing happens.

When you are handed a failing patient, this list is your differential.

Nursing

Name which part of the loop is broken and you know which sign to watch for, and which sign is never going to show up.

Example: In a patient on a beta blocker, do not wait for a rising heart rate to tell you they are bleeding. Watch the other signs.

Radiologic technology

Reactions on the table fall into two groups depending on which pathway is firing, and the two look nothing alike.

Example: A patient who goes pale, sweaty and slow-pulsed after an injection is a vagal reaction: the efferent nerve output dropped the heart rate. Anaphylaxis drives the heart rate up instead. Same table, opposite pathways, opposite responses from you.

Medicine

The seven boxes give you a list of suspects. Each broken box produces its own picture.

Example: A patient who cannot raise their heart rate while bleeding may have an intact sensor and a blocked efferent path, which is exactly what a beta blocker does.

Respiratory therapy

Nearly everything you do starts with a chemoreceptor noticing something. That is the sensor box of the loop.

Example: If the sensor is blunted, the drive to breathe falls even though the stimulus telling it to rise is still there.

16

Control

Negative feedback, worked

Response
  • Negative feedback means the response opposes the stimulus that produced it.
  • It stabilizes. It cannot prevent the initial change, only correct it, which is why the variable oscillates rather than sitting still.
  • Negative feedback loops shut themselves off. That is the whole point of the word negative.

Predict

You wrap the skin thermoreceptors so they read warm while the core is actually cooling. Which of the seven boxes is broken, and what does core temperature do over the next hour?

Nursing

A body that is still fixing the problem looks different from one that has run out of room. Telling those apart is most of assessment.

Example: A brief drop on standing that recovers is the loop working. A drop that stays down is the loop failing.

Radiologic technology

Every time you sit a patient up or lay them flat you are running this loop live and watching what it does.

Example: Sitting a patient up from a supine scan, or standing them for an upright film, drops their pressure for a few seconds before the reflex catches it. In an older or dehydrated patient the catch is slow, and that is the moment they go down. It is why you move them in stages and stay with them.

Medicine

Negative feedback cannot stop a change from happening. It can only pull it back. So the value always moves first.

Example: Blood pressure dips on standing before the reflex catches it. The dip is normal. Failing to recover from it is not.

Respiratory therapy

Breathing harder is the body's negative feedback answer to carbon dioxide going up.

Example: Rising carbon dioxide normally drives ventilation up. If the carbon dioxide is high and ventilation is not rising, the loop is broken somewhere.

17

Control

Positive feedback and feedforward

Mechanism

Positive feedback

  • The response reinforces the stimulus, driving the variable further from where it started.
  • It is destabilizing by design, so it needs an outside event to stop it.
  • Examples: oxytocin in labor, stopped by delivery. The clotting cascade, stopped when the vessel seals. Sodium entry in an action potential, stopped by channel inactivation.
  • Positive feedback with no off switch is a description of dying. Uncontrolled bleeding and runaway hyperkalemia both work this way.

Feedforward control

  • The body responds to a signal that predicts a change, before the regulated variable has moved at all.
  • Examples: salivating and secreting gastric acid at the smell of food. Heart rate rising in the seconds before exercise starts.
  • Feedforward buys time. It cannot correct an error because there is no error yet.

Keep this

Negative feedback reacts to what happened. Feedforward reacts to what is about to happen. Positive feedback commits to what is already happening.

Nursing

Catching a self-reinforcing spiral early is the reason for frequent reassessment.

Example: A patient trending the same direction on every set of vitals is in a loop, not having one bad reading.

Radiologic technology

Panic in a closed scanner is a positive feedback loop, and nothing inside the bore will break it.

Example: Anxiety speeds the breathing, low carbon dioxide causes tingling and lightheadedness, the strange sensations raise the anxiety, and it goes around again. Your voice on the intercom is the outside event that stops it, which is why talking the patient through is a technique and not a courtesy.

Medicine

Positive feedback has no built-in stop, so it needs an event from outside the loop to end it.

Example: Bleeding lowers pressure, which lowers perfusion to the heart, which lowers output, which lowers pressure again. Nothing inside that loop stops it.

Respiratory therapy

Air trapping feeds itself.

Example: Incomplete exhalation raises the trapped volume, which shortens the time available to exhale, which traps more air on the next breath.

18

Control

Set points move

Perturb
  • Fever is not a broken thermostat. It is a thermostat deliberately reset upward by pyrogens, which is why you shiver while your temperature is already climbing.
  • Circadian rhythms move set points on a daily cycle. Core temperature, cortisol and blood pressure all have a normal range that depends on the time of day.
  • Acclimatization resets over days to weeks: hematocrit at altitude, sweat sodium in a hot climate.

Perturb

A patient with a fever is given a drug that resets the set point back to 37 C (98.6 F) while their core is at 39 C (102.2 F). Predict what the body does in the next twenty minutes, and name which effectors it uses.

Nursing

Cool a patient who is shivering and you are fighting the thermostat instead of turning it down.

Example: Shivering generates heat, so external cooling during the rise can drive the temperature higher rather than lower.

Radiologic technology

Scanner rooms are kept cold for the machine. The patient's thermostat reacts to that whether you want it to or not.

Example: A cold, uncovered patient shivers, and shivering is movement, and movement is motion artifact. The warm blanket is an image quality tool, not just a comfort measure.

Medicine

In a fever the thermostat is not broken. It has been turned up, and the body is working hard to reach the new number.

Example: The patient shivers while their temperature is already climbing, because the body reads its current temperature as too cold against the new target.

Respiratory therapy

If carbon dioxide stays high for long enough, the sensor stops treating it as an emergency and settles on a new normal.

Example: In long-standing carbon dioxide retention the body adapts to a higher normal, so the textbook number is no longer that patient's target.

Science of physiology

How we know any of this

Mechanism

Terminology

  • Independent variable: what the experimenter changes.
  • Dependent variable: what is measured in response.
  • Control: an otherwise identical condition where the independent variable is not changed.
  • Placebo: an inactive treatment used to separate the effect of the substance from the effect of being treated.
  • Blind and double blind: who knows which group is which. Double blind means neither the participant nor the person measuring knows.

Why human physiology is hard to study

  • Variability between people is large and mostly normal, not error.
  • A single value tells you far less than a trend. This is why we chart.
  • Reference ranges are usually set to cover the middle 95 percent of a healthy population, so one healthy person in twenty falls outside a given range by definition.

Consequence

Run a twenty-analyte panel on a perfectly healthy person and the odds are close to even that at least one result comes back flagged. A flagged value is a reason to look, not a diagnosis. In your patient file you get new numbers for the same person every week, and one week on its own never tells you the story.

Nursing

One number on its own tells you very little. What that number did since last time tells you a lot.

Example: A creatinine at the top of normal that doubled since yesterday is a bigger problem than one that is mildly high and unchanged.

Radiologic technology

You check lab numbers before giving contrast. A number outside the normal range does not automatically mean stop.

Example: A creatinine sitting slightly high and unchanged for a year is a different situation from one that doubled this week. Both print with the same flag on the report. Only one of them changes what happens next.

Medicine

Normal ranges are built to cover most healthy people, not every healthy person. So about one healthy person in twenty gets flagged on any given test.

Example: Run a twenty-analyte panel on a completely healthy person and something comes back flagged more often than not.

Respiratory therapy

A blood gas is one photo of something that is still moving.

Example: The same value on the way up and on the way down mean opposite things about where the patient is heading.
