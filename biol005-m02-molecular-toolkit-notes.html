Build the Molecular Toolkit

Every box in the control loop you drew last time was carried out by a molecule. This is the toolkit, and the rule that runs through all of it is that shape decides function. Silverthorn chapter 2.

Competencies 1 to 4 of week 2

Atoms, ions and charge

Ion
An atom or molecule with a net electrical charge, because it has gained or lost electrons.
Cation
A positively charged ion. Na+, K+, Ca2+, H+.
Anion
A negatively charged ion. Cl-, HCO3-, phosphate, protein.
Electrolyte
A substance that separates into ions in solution and therefore conducts current.
Trace element
An element needed in very small amounts, usually because it is a cofactor. Iron, iodine, zinc, copper.
Free radical
A molecule with an unpaired electron, unstable and reactive. Relevant later to cell injury.

Why charge matters more than anything else here

A charged particle cannot cross the oily interior of a membrane on its own. Every ion that moves in the body moves through a protein. That single fact generates most of Weeks 3 through 5.

The ions to know now, and where each one shows up
IonTypical plasma valueWhere it matters
Sodium, Na+135 to 145 mMECF volume, membrane potential, most secondary active transport
Potassium, K+3.5 to 5.0 mMResting membrane potential, cardiac excitability
Calcium, Ca2+2.2 to 2.6 mM totalMuscle contraction, secretion, intracellular signaling
Chloride, Cl-98 to 107 mMMain ECF anion, inhibition in neurons, gastric acid
Bicarbonate, HCO3-22 to 26 mMThe principal buffer in blood
Hydrogen, H+about 40 nMSets pH, and therefore the shape of every protein

Note the units on hydrogen ion. It is present at roughly a millionth the concentration of sodium, and it is defended more tightly than anything else on the list.

Nursing

Potassium matters more than the other electrolytes because the heart's ability to fire depends on it.

Example: Potassium is replaced with monitoring and never pushed fast, because the margin between correcting it and causing an arrhythmia is small.

Radiologic technology

Ionizing radiation is named for exactly this. The beam knocks electrons off atoms in tissue and leaves charged particles behind.

Example: Most of that happens in water, because water is most of the tissue. The reactive fragments it makes are what go on to damage DNA, and that chain is the entire reason dose limits, shielding and time-distance-shielding exist.

Medicine

A charged particle cannot get through a membrane by itself. Every ion that moves needs a protein to go through.

Example: That is why a drug that blocks one channel can change the behavior of an entire organ.

Respiratory therapy

Electrolytes decide how hard a muscle can pull, and breathing is muscle work.

Example: Low potassium or low phosphate weakens the diaphragm, which shows up as a failed weaning trial rather than as breathlessness.

Water and solubility

Polar molecule
A molecule with an uneven distribution of charge, so one region is slightly negative and another slightly positive. Water is the important example.
Hydrogen bond
A weak attraction between the slightly positive hydrogen of one polar molecule and a slightly negative atom on another. Weak alone, powerful in bulk.
Hydrophilic
Dissolves readily in water. Polar or charged molecules.
Hydrophobic
Does not dissolve in water. Nonpolar molecules, mostly carbon and hydrogen.
Amphipathic
Carrying both a polar region and a nonpolar region in one molecule. Phospholipids are the reason membranes exist.
Solvent and solute
The solvent does the dissolving, the solute is what dissolves. In the body the solvent is essentially always water.

A prediction you can make from this alone

A hydrophilic drug dissolves easily in plasma and crosses membranes with difficulty. A hydrophobic drug crosses membranes easily but needs a carrier protein to travel in blood. Before knowing anything else about a molecule, its polarity tells you a great deal about where it can go and how it gets there.

Nursing

Whether a drug dissolves is why some of them need a certain fluid, a filter, or a line of their own.

Example: Oily preparations separate out or stick to tubing, so the route matters as much as the dose.

Radiologic technology

MRI is not really imaging tissue. It is imaging hydrogen, and nearly all the hydrogen in a person is sitting in water and fat.

Example: Two soft tissues look different on MRI because they hold different amounts of water and fat. That is why MRI separates soft tissues an X-ray cannot tell apart, and why a dense bone that dominates a radiograph can be nearly silent on MRI.

Medicine

Whether a molecule likes water or likes oil tells you where in the body it can get to.

Example: A fat soluble drug crosses into the brain readily. A water soluble one largely does not, which is why two similar drugs can have very different effects on alertness.

Respiratory therapy

An inhaled drug has to hold together as droplets the whole way down and still reach the small airways.

Example: Particle size and humidity change how much of a nebulized dose gets past the upper airway at all.

Saying how much

Molarity = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution

Units: mol/L, written M. Physiological values are usually mM.

What it says: molarity counts particles, not mass. Equal masses of two different solutes are different numbers of molecules, and it is the number of molecules that produces the effect.

Equivalents = moles × charge per ion

Used for ions, because charge balance matters as much as particle count.

What it says: one mole of Ca2+ carries twice the charge of one mole of Na+. Body fluids stay electrically neutral overall, so ions move in matched sets and you cannot change one in isolation.

pH = −log[H+]

The logarithm is the point. One pH unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.

What it says: a pH change that looks small is not small. A fall from 7.4 to 7.1 roughly doubles hydrogen ion concentration, which is enough to change protein shape throughout the body.

Acid
A molecule that releases H+ into solution.
Base
A molecule that accepts H+ from solution.
Buffer
A system that resists a change in pH by binding H+ when it is added and releasing it when it is removed. The bicarbonate system is the main one in blood.
Acidosis and alkalosis
Plasma pH below 7.35 and above 7.45. Both are defined against the arterial value, not the cellular one.

Nursing

The first sign of an acid problem is often how the person is acting, not a number on a report.

Example: New confusion or agitation can arrive before anyone has drawn a gas.

Radiologic technology

The body holds its pH in a very narrow window, so anything you inject has to be mixed to match it.

Example: Older high-osmolar agents sat further from the body's own chemistry, and patients felt it as heat, pain and far more reactions. Modern low-osmolar agents are tolerated better because the chemistry is closer to what the blood already is.

Medicine

A pH change that looks tiny is not tiny. Every whole step on the scale is ten times more acid.

Example: Falling from 7.4 to 7.1 roughly doubles the hydrogen ion concentration, which is enough to change protein shape throughout the body.

Respiratory therapy

Reading acid and base status is the main skill of the entire specialty.

Example: Ventilation is the fast lever on pH, so a change in minute ventilation moves the pH within minutes, not hours.

The four families

Carbohydrate
Built from C, H and O in roughly a 1:2:1 ratio. Fast fuel, and a labeling system on cell surfaces.
Monosaccharide
A single sugar unit. Glucose, fructose, galactose.
Polysaccharide
Many sugar units joined. Glycogen is the animal storage form, stored in liver and muscle.
Lipid
Largely carbon and hydrogen, so nonpolar and not water soluble.
Triglyceride
Three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone. The dense energy store.
Phospholipid
Two fatty acid tails plus a phosphate head. Amphipathic, and therefore the basis of every membrane.
Steroid
A lipid built on four linked carbon rings, derived from cholesterol. Cortisol, aldosterone, the sex hormones.
Nucleotide
A sugar, a base and one or more phosphate groups. ATP, cAMP, NAD, FAD, and the units of DNA and RNA.

Proteins: shape decides everything

Amino acid
The building block of protein. A central carbon carrying an amino group, a carboxyl group, and a variable side chain. Twenty are used.
Side chain
The variable part of an amino acid. Whether a region of a protein ends up polar, charged or oily is decided here.
Peptide bond
The covalent bond joining one amino acid to the next.
Primary structure
The amino acid sequence.
Secondary structure
Local folding into alpha helices and beta sheets, held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
Tertiary structure
The complete three-dimensional shape of a single chain.
Quaternary structure
Two or more chains assembled into one functional protein. Hemoglobin has four.
Denaturation
Loss of the folded shape from heat, extreme pH, or certain chemicals. Function is lost with it, and often does not return.

Nursing

Keeping a patient's temperature in range is really about protecting their proteins.

Example: Above roughly 40 C (104 F) the concern shifts from comfort to enzymes losing their shape.

Radiologic technology

MRI puts radio energy into the patient, and that energy turns into heat in the tissue.

Example: The SAR limit the scanner enforces on a sequence exists for this reason. It is a cap on how much the patient is allowed to warm, because tissue proteins have a temperature they will not survive.

Medicine

When a protein loses its folded shape it loses its job, and often it does not get that job back.

Example: That is why a very high body temperature is an emergency in its own right and not just an uncomfortable symptom.

Respiratory therapy

Heated humidifiers have a ceiling on purpose, because too hot burns the tissue you are trying to help.

Example: Delivered gas is warmed to body temperature and no higher, because airway proteins denature the same way any other protein does.

Binding, the mechanism under all of it

Ligand
Any molecule that binds to a protein. A substrate is the ligand of an enzyme, a hormone the ligand of a receptor.
Binding site
The pocket on the protein whose shape and charge distribution match the ligand.
Specificity
How selective a binding site is about what it accepts. High specificity means it refuses close chemical relatives.
Affinity
How tightly the site holds what it has bound. High affinity means the complex lasts longer before dissociating.
Competition
Two molecules similar enough to fit the same site, so each reduces the binding of the other.
Saturation
The point at which every binding site is occupied and adding more ligand produces no further increase.

Specificity and affinity are independent

A site can be extremely fussy about what it accepts and still hold it loosely. Another can accept several related molecules and hold each one tightly. Do not use the two words interchangeably, and expect exam questions that depend on the difference.

Where saturation shows up clinically

  • Glucose appears in urine only once plasma glucose passes roughly 180 mg/dL, because the renal transporters saturate. Below that they reabsorb everything.
  • Hemoglobin saturates with oxygen, which is why raising inspired oxygen helps a hypoxic patient far less once saturation is already near maximum.
  • Drug metabolism saturates, which is why some drugs move from predictable dosing to dangerous accumulation over a narrow range.

Nursing

The curve is steep in the middle and flat at the top. So the same size drop means something very different depending on where you started.

Example: A drop from 99 to 95 percent is small. The same four point drop from 90 to 86 is not.

Radiologic technology

Detectors fill up the same way proteins do. Past a point, more input buys you nothing.

Example: An overexposed region of a digital image is saturated: every pixel there is already reading maximum, so the detail is gone and more dose will not bring it back. It only adds exposure to the patient for no image.

Medicine

If something in the body saturates, a protein is behind it. And saturation gives you a cliff rather than a slow drift.

Example: Glucose appears in the urine only once the transporters are overwhelmed, which is why there is a threshold instead of a steady trickle.

Respiratory therapy

The oxygen and hemoglobin curve you will work with every day is exactly this shape.

Example: Above about 90 percent, adding more oxygen buys very little extra saturation, so the fix usually has to be somewhere other than the oxygen dial.

Turning proteins up and down

Cofactor
A non-protein helper a protein needs in order to function. Often a metal ion such as iron or zinc.
Coenzyme
An organic cofactor, usually derived from a vitamin. This is why a single vitamin deficiency can produce effects across many systems.
Allosteric modulator
A molecule that binds somewhere other than the active site and changes the shape of that site. Can activate or inhibit.
Covalent modulator
A group attached to the protein, most often a phosphate, switching activity on or off. Phosphorylation is the body's main switch.
Competitive inhibitor
A molecule that binds the active site itself. Because it competes, enough substrate can outcompete it.
Proenzyme or zymogen
An inactive precursor activated later by cutting part of the molecule away. Pepsinogen becomes pepsin in stomach acid.
Isozyme
One of several forms of the same enzyme catalyzing the same reaction, often tissue specific. The basis of cardiac enzyme testing.

Worked clinical case

Methanol poisoning is treated with ethanol or fomepizole. Both compete for alcohol dehydrogenase, the enzyme that would otherwise convert methanol into formic acid. It works because the inhibition is competitive: flood the enzyme with a rival substrate and the rival wins. An allosteric inhibitor could not be treated that way, because more substrate does not restore a site whose shape has already changed.

Nursing

A competitive blocker can be pushed off by more of the real thing, and that changes how a reversal behaves over time.

Example: As a reversal agent clears, the original drug can win the site back, so the patient has to be watched past the first improvement.

Radiologic technology

Premedication before contrast works by getting to the site first, which is why the timing is not negotiable.

Example: Diphenhydramine and steroids are given to occupy the sites histamine would use, ahead of the injection. A competitive blocker has to already be sitting on the receptor when the histamine arrives. Given afterward it is competing against a site that is already taken.

Medicine

Whether a blocker is competitive decides whether adding more of the real thing can beat it.

Example: Methanol poisoning is treated by flooding the same enzyme with a rival substrate, and that only works because the block is competitive.

Respiratory therapy

Some drugs bind somewhere else entirely, and no amount of the real thing will push them off.

Example: Adding more of the natural signal will not restore function if the active site itself has already changed shape.

Reactions and mass action

A + B ⇌ AB

The double arrow is the whole point. Reversible reactions run in both directions at once.

What it says: add more of anything on one side and the reaction shifts away from it. Remove something and it shifts toward that side to replace it. This one idea explains oxygen loading in the lung, carbon dioxide carriage in blood, and every buffer in the body.

  • Reaction rate depends on reactant concentration, temperature, and the amount of enzyme present.
  • Enzymes speed a reaction by lowering the activation energy. They do not change the direction, are not consumed, and cannot make an unfavorable reaction favorable.
  • Activation energy is the energy barrier a reaction has to clear before it can proceed.

Carry this into Week 13

Carbon dioxide plus water forms carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. Every step is reversible. When a patient stops breathing adequately, carbon dioxide accumulates, the reaction shifts to the right, hydrogen ion rises and pH falls. You can predict respiratory acidosis from mass action alone, months before you study the respiratory system.

Nursing

How fast someone breathes is a pH control, not only an oxygen control.

Example: A patient who stops breathing adequately becomes acidotic within minutes, well before any lab result returns.

Radiologic technology

The breath hold you coach on every chest CT is carbon dioxide piling up, and the patient is losing that race.

Example: Holding a breath lets carbon dioxide build, the reaction shifts toward acid, and that shift is what the patient feels as the urge to breathe. Coaching a few deep breaths first drives carbon dioxide down before the hold, which buys a longer and steadier hold and a sharper image.

Medicine

Push on one side of a reversible reaction and the whole thing slides the other way.

Example: Carbon dioxide accumulating shifts the reaction toward acid, and the pH falls as a direct consequence.

Respiratory therapy

Changing how much air someone moves each minute is the fastest way to change their blood pH.

Example: Change ventilation, carbon dioxide changes, and pH changes with it, all inside a few minutes.

Water and solution properties

Explain how the polarity and hydrogen bonding of water determine solubility and identify whether a given solute is hydrophilic or hydrophobic.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

1-1

Draw a water molecule and label the partial charges on it. Beside it draw one thing that dissolves in body water and one thing that does not, and write one sentence under each saying why. Then write what the body has to do for the one that does not dissolve.

1-2

Draw a blood vessel in cross section. Show one substance traveling free in the plasma and one traveling bound to a carrier protein. Label both, and write what decides which way a substance has to travel.

02

pH and buffers

Define pH, state the normal pH range of arterial blood, and explain how a buffer pair resists a change in pH when acid or base is added.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

2-1

Write the bicarbonate buffer equation from memory. Draw an arrow showing which way it shifts when acid is added and mark what happens to bicarbonate. Then write what the lung does to this equation and what the kidney does, and how fast each one works.

2-2

Draw the pH scale from 6.8 to 7.8. Mark the normal arterial range, mark which end is acidaemia and which is alkalaemia, and mark the survival limits. Under it write the Henderson equation with the normal numbers filled in, and show that it gives 7.4.

03

Protein structure and function

Relate the levels of protein structure to binding site shape and explain how denaturation by heat or pH change destroys function.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

3-1

Draw the four levels of protein structure as four small sketches in order and label each one. On the tertiary sketch mark where the binding site is, and write one sentence saying why the shape is the job rather than a consequence of the job.

3-2

Draw a folded protein with its binding site. Then draw it again after heat, and again after a pH change. Label what happened to the fold each time, and write what the protein can no longer do and whether it can recover.

04

Enzyme activity and regulation

Describe how enzymes lower activation energy and predict the effect of substrate concentration, temperature, pH, and competitive or allosteric inhibition on reaction rate.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

4-1

Draw rate against substrate concentration and label Vmax and Km. Then draw the same axes twice more, once with a competitive inhibitor added and once with an allosteric one. Beside each, write what happened to Vmax, what happened to Km, and whether adding more substrate helps.

4-2

Draw rate against temperature, and rate against pH, on separate axes. Mark the optimum on each. Beside each curve write what is happening on the way up and what is happening on the way down, and say which side of the peak can be reversed and which cannot.
