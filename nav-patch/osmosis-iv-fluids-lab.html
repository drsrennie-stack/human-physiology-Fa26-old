Osmosis and IV Fluids Lab

Start here

Water has no opinion. It goes where the solute is.

Water crosses cell membranes freely, almost everywhere in the body, through aquaporins and through the lipid bilayer itself. It is not pumped and it is not chosen. It moves down its own concentration gradient, which is the same as saying it moves toward wherever solute is more concentrated, until the concentrations on both sides match.

That single sentence predicts every result in this lab. If you know what a fluid does to the solute concentration outside the cells, you already know which way water will move and roughly how much. What you do not get to do is memorize a list of which bag goes where, because the list falls apart the moment the patient is not a healthy adult with a normal sodium.

The compartments, in a 70 kg adult

These fractions are teaching approximations. They shift with age, sex, and body composition, because fat holds much less water than muscle does.

  • Total body water is about 60 percent of body weight in an adult man and about 50 percent in an adult woman, so roughly 42 liters here. Older adults run lower, closer to 50 and 45 percent.
  • Two thirds sits inside cells, about 28 liters. This is the intracellular compartment, and it is the reservoir that hypotonic and hypertonic fluids move water into and out of.
  • One third sits outside cells, about 14 liters. Of that, three quarters is interstitial fluid bathing the tissues and one quarter, about 3.5 liters, is plasma.

That last number is why a liter of the wrong fluid does almost nothing for a patient who is bleeding. Only the plasma fraction is holding up the blood pressure.

Osmolarity is a number. Tonicity is a behavior.

Osmolarity counts every dissolved particle in a liter, whether or not it can cross the membrane. You can calculate the effective part from a chemistry panel as twice the sodium plus the glucose divided by 18.

Tonicity is what a solution actually does to a cell sitting in it, and only particles that cannot cross the membrane count. A solute that crosses freely equilibrates on both sides and ends up pulling on nothing.

Urea is the standard example. It raises measured osmolarity, it crosses cell membranes readily, and at steady state it moves no water. Dextrose in a bag of D5W is the same story for a different reason: it is an effective osmole in the bag, and then cells take it up and burn it, and what is left behind is plain water. This is why the bag label and the physiological effect disagree, and why students who memorize the label get D5W wrong every time.

Section 2

What is actually in the bag

Two columns matter more than the rest. The bag osmolarity is what a machine measures in the fluid before it goes in. The effective osmolarity is what is still pulling on water once the body has had a few minutes with it, and it is the number that predicts where the water lands. They differ whenever the bag contains dextrose, because cells take dextrose up and metabolize it, leaving the water behind.

Look at plasma in the last row while you read. Normal saline is called normal, and its chloride is more than half again the plasma value. That difference has consequences, and you meet them in order 3.

Why dextrose is in the bag at all

Fifty grams of dextrose is only about 170 kilocalories, nowhere near a day's needs, so it is not feeding anyone. It is there to blunt ketosis. A small steady glucose supply keeps insulin from bottoming out, and that is enough to slow the breakdown of muscle protein for fuel in a patient who is not eating. It also means a fluid that started as sugar water ends as plain water, which is the whole trick of D5W.

Why the chloride number matters

Plasma chloride runs about 100 mEq/L and normal saline delivers 154. Give enough of it and extracellular chloride climbs. As chloride rises relative to sodium, bicarbonate has to fall to keep the charges balanced, and the patient develops an acidosis with a normal anion gap. Lactated Ringer's avoids this by carrying less chloride and replacing the difference with lactate, which the liver converts to bicarbonate.

      Section 4

      The compartment model

      This runs the same two compartment osmotic calculation on any patient and any fluid, and it is worth understanding rather than just using. There are only four steps in it.

      1. Work out the patient's total body water from weight, and split it two thirds inside cells and one third outside.
      2. Work out the starting effective osmolarity from sodium and glucose, and multiply by total body water to get the total effective solute the patient is carrying.
      3. Add the fluid. Its volume goes into total body water and its effective osmoles go into total solute. Divide one by the other for the new osmolarity.
      4. Cell solute did not change, because nothing in these bags crosses into cells and stays there. So the new intracellular volume is old cell solute divided by the new osmolarity, and whatever is left of total body water is extracellular.

      That last step is the whole thing. Water moves until concentrations match, so the cells swell or shrink by exactly the amount that restores equality, and you can calculate it rather than guess at it.

      What this model does not do

      • It assumes the kidneys are not excreting anything during the infusion. Over hours that is wrong, and a working kidney will blunt everything you see here.
      • It treats the plasma share of the extracellular space as a fixed quarter. Capillary leak in sepsis and low albumin in liver disease both push more of it into the interstitium than that.
      • It does not model oncotic pressure, so it will understate what albumin does to plasma volume. Albumin is flagged separately in the results for that reason.
      • It says nothing about whether a heart, a brain or a lung can tolerate the shift it predicts. That judgment is yours and it is where the orders in section 3 live.

      A model you can state the limits of is a model you understand. Being able to name these is worth points in the discussion.

      Section 5

      Build it backward from the consequence

      Solving a case and building one are not the same skill, and the second is harder. You will be assigned a consequence, something that actually went wrong in a patient. Your job is to work backward and construct the patient and the fluid order that would produce it, run your own numbers through the model in section 4 to show it is physiologically possible, and then say what should have been ordered instead.

      This is the reverse of what you did in section 3, and it is much harder to fake. A consequence has many possible causes and only some of them survive the arithmetic.

        Section 6

        The flow chart

        After eleven orders you know something you cannot easily say out loud, which is the order in which you actually check things before you agree to hang a bag. The flow chart is where that becomes visible. You are not drawing a summary of IV fluids. You are drawing the decision path you personally used, in the sequence you used it, with the questions written the way you asked them.

        Your path audit from section 3 gets submitted alongside the drawing, and the two have to agree.

        Required decision points

        Your chart has to branch on all six of these somewhere, in whatever order you found yourself using them.

        1. Which compartment is the patient actually short in, or overloaded in. Nothing else can be decided before this one.
        2. What is the fluid's effective osmolarity compared with the patient's. Not the bag label, the effective number.
        3. Which way will water move, and how much. Into cells, out of cells, or neither.
        4. How fast is anything allowed to change. Especially sodium, in either direction.
        5. What comes along with the water. Chloride, potassium, dextrose, lactate.
        6. Can this patient's heart, brain and lungs tolerate the shift you just predicted.

        Requirements

        • Hand drawn on paper, or drawn in the course canvas in section 7 and exported as the stamped PDF. Both are worth the same.
        • Every branch is a question with a yes or no, or a small set of exclusive answers. No branch may be a topic heading.
        • At least eight of the eleven orders end somewhere on your chart, and you label those endpoints.
        • At least one endpoint says stop and do not give this, rather than naming a fluid.
        • Mark the point where the choice of fluid stops mattering and the rate becomes the whole question.
        • Sign it with your name.

        Two ways to submit the drawing

        Route A. Paper

        Draw it on paper in pen or pencil, photograph it, and post the photo. Cross outs and arrows squeezed into the margin are welcome, they are evidence of thinking rather than sloppiness.

        Route B. The course canvas, section 7

        Draw it in the canvas built into this lab and export the stamped PDF. The canvas cannot open, paste or import a picture, so every mark in that PDF was made stroke by stroke inside the tool, and the stamp on the last page records how it was built.

        What is not accepted. A drawing made in any other digital app, including a tablet drawing app, a diagramming tool or anything AI generated. The reason is practical rather than suspicious. A finished digital image gives me no way to tell a drawing from a generated picture, and both of the routes above do.

        Where AI helps here, and where it cannot follow

        Use it for

        • Asking why a mechanism works the way it does, after you have committed your own read.
        • Arguing the other side. Have it defend the fluid you rejected as strongly as it can, then decide whether it beat you.
        • Attacking your built case. Ask it to find the contradiction, and if it finds one, fix it.
        • Rehearsing your walkthrough and telling you where it was unclear.

        It cannot produce

        • Your path audit, which is a record of what you did, order by order.
        • A drawing in your hand on paper, or one built stroke by stroke in the canvas.
        • An account of what changed in your own reasoning, which is the graded half of every order.
        • Your seeded consequence, since no two students have the same one.
        • A live unscripted walkthrough of a patient moving through your chart.

        Try this and watch what happens. Take order 6, the one where a colleague objects to lactated Ringer's because it contains potassium, and put that objection to an AI as though you believed it. Then take order 7 and ask what dose of sterile water would be safe to infuse. You are required to include both exchanges in your log. A model that agrees with a confident wrong premise is showing you its most important failure mode, and in a clinical setting that failure mode has a body attached to it.

        The discussion

        1. Post your chart. Photo or stamped PDF, plus a Canvas Studio video of three minutes or less. Walk one of the eleven patients through your chart out loud, from the first question to the endpoint. Do not summarize what the chart contains, drive a patient through it.
        2. Post your built case. Patient and order only. Do not name your consequence yet.
        3. Reply to two classmates. Run their case through your own chart and say what complication you land on. Then say where their chart would struggle, using a specific order it cannot resolve.
        4. Close your own thread. Reveal your consequence, say whether your classmates landed on it, and describe one change you would make to your chart based on what came back.

        Every chart in this class breaks somewhere. Finding out where yours breaks, in public, with a real case, is what makes the next version good. Nobody loses points for a chart that turns out to have a gap. Points come off for a chart nobody could break because it never committed to anything.
