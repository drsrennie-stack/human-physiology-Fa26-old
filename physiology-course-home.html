What this course actually asks of you

Not to memorize physiology. To be able to produce it.

Physiology is mechanism. Almost every question in this course is some version of the same one: something changed, what does the body do about it, and why does that work.

So what gets assessed is whether you can build the explanation yourself, from a blank page, out loud. Recognizing a correct answer on a list is not the same skill, and it is not the one this course is measuring. That sounds harder than it is, because the whole week is built to get you there.

There are 268 competencies in this course, each one a specific thing you will be able to do. Nothing is graded that is not on that list, and you can read the whole list any time.

How you are graded

Four categories, each named for what it asks you to do.

35%

Show Me What You Know

Two exams, 17.5 percent each. You draw a physiological pathway and teach it out loud on video, with no notes.

25%

Investigate It

The weekly labs, most of them in PhysioEx, written up the way a clinical write up is written.

25%

Use It

Your weekly application case, chosen from several set in different rooms. These build into your patient file.

15%

Think About It

One discussion a week, carrying the physiology and your thinking about it together.

Who is teaching this

A decade of teaching the medical sciences, and years in clinical medicine before that.

I'm Dr. Sharilyn Rennie. I have taught anatomy, physiology and the medical sciences for over a decade, and before teaching I spent years in clinical medicine. That background shapes how I teach: every mechanism here connects to something that actually happens in a body, usually to somebody who needs it to go right.

Physiology is how the body holds itself together. My job is to help you see the logic, one week at a time, with real data to reason through rather than lists to hold in your head.

This course is designed to be demanding and flexible at the same time. You are accountable for showing me you have mastered the material. How you get there, and when in the week you do it, is largely yours to choose.

Every system tells a story. We follow the mechanism together.

Questions you probably have right now

The short answers. There is a longer list if you want it.

What should I do first?

Open Start Here. It walks you through the course, how grading works, and your introduction post, and then sends you to Week 1. It takes about ten minutes.

It's online. When do I actually work?

Whenever fits your life. Nothing meets at a set time and there are no live sessions. Everything for a week is due Sunday at 10:00 pm, with the discussion's first post due Friday.

Plan on roughly 16 to 20 hours a week to genuinely learn this material. That is not a scare number; it is what it takes to walk into an exam able to draw a pathway and teach it with no notes.

How do the exams work?

Two midterms, 17.5 percent each, and neither is multiple choice. You draw a physiological pathway and teach it on video, out loud, with no notes.

Each one is a three day window rather than a fixed hour, so you choose your time inside it. Midterm 1 covers Weeks 1 to 7 and runs October 26 to 28. Midterm 2 covers Weeks 8 to 14 and runs December 14 to 16. Every week includes ungraded practice in exactly that format.

Do I need to buy anything?

Yes, and there is one right way to do it. Open our Canvas course, click Access Pearson in the left menu, and purchase there. That single path gets you PhysioEx, the labs, and the free eText of the book. Set it up in Week 1, because the labs are 25 percent of your grade and they live inside it.

What if I fall behind?

Tell me early. Message me in the Canvas Inbox or email srennie@yccd.edu, and come to office hours, Wednesdays 9:00 to 10:00 am on Zoom, drop in.

Falling behind in a course like this is almost always a method problem rather than an effort problem, and method problems are fixable in about a week. The thing that makes them unfixable is waiting until after an exam to mention it.

Can I use AI?

On some things, yes, with disclosure. On others, no. The line is whether the work is meant to be evidence of your own reasoning.
