Start

Last time you drew a control loop. Every box in it was done by a molecule.

  • A sensor is a protein that changes shape when something binds it.
  • A signal is a molecule that fits a receptor and nothing else.
  • An effector is a protein that converts binding into physical work.
  • So the question today is what makes one molecule able to do a job another cannot.

Silverthorn chapter 2. Course competencies 1, 2 and 3.

The problem

Assemble a toolkit of molecules capable of sensing, signaling, transporting and doing work, and explain what gives each one its job.

The problem

Two molecules, one job

Problem

These two are close relatives. One raises blood glucose, the other lowers it, and the difference is entirely shape.

Why can they not substitute for each other?
InsulinGlucagon
Chemical classPeptide hormonePeptide hormone
Made inPancreatic beta cellsPancreatic alpha cells
Travels inPlasma, same routePlasma, same route

The shape of the answer

Same class, same source organ, same delivery route, opposite effects. The only thing left that can differ is which receptor each one fits, and what that receptor is wired to do. Specificity is not a property of the hormone alone. It is a property of the pair.

03

Atoms and ions

The ions that run physiology

Mechanism
  • An ion is an atom or molecule carrying a net charge, because it gained or lost electrons.
  • Cations are positive, anions are negative.
  • Charge is what makes an ion unable to cross a lipid membrane on its own. Every ion that moves needs a protein to move through.
The short list worth memorizing now
IonWhere it matters
Na+ECF volume, membrane potential, most secondary transport
K+Resting membrane potential, cardiac excitability
Ca2+Muscle contraction, secretion, signaling
Cl-ECF anion, inhibition in neurons, gastric acid
H+pH, so the shape of every protein
HCO3-The main buffer in blood

Nursing

Potassium matters more than the other electrolytes because the heart's ability to fire depends on it.

Example: Potassium is replaced with monitoring and never pushed fast, because the margin between correcting it and causing an arrhythmia is small.

Radiologic technology

Ionizing radiation is named for exactly this. The beam knocks electrons off atoms in tissue and leaves charged particles behind.

Example: Most of that happens in water, because water is most of the tissue. The reactive fragments it makes are what go on to damage DNA, and that chain is the entire reason dose limits, shielding and time-distance-shielding exist.

Medicine

A charged particle cannot get through a membrane by itself. Every ion that moves needs a protein to go through.

Example: That is why a drug that blocks one channel can change the behavior of an entire organ.

Respiratory therapy

Electrolytes decide how hard a muscle can pull, and breathing is muscle work.

Example: Low potassium or low phosphate weakens the diaphragm, which shows up as a failed weaning trial rather than as breathlessness.

04

Water

Polarity is why anything dissolves

Mechanism
  • Water is polar: oxygen pulls the shared electrons, so oxygen is slightly negative and the hydrogens slightly positive.
  • That partial charge lets water molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other and with other polar molecules.
  • Individually a hydrogen bond is weak. In bulk they set water's boiling point, its surface tension, and its ability to carry heat.
  • Water is the solvent for essentially every reaction in the body, so solubility in water decides where a molecule can go.

Three words you will use all term

  • Hydrophilic: dissolves readily in water. Polar or charged.
  • Hydrophobic: does not. Nonpolar, mostly carbon and hydrogen.
  • Amphipathic: both ends in one molecule, a polar head and nonpolar tails. Phospholipids are amphipathic, which is the entire reason a membrane exists.

Application

A hydrophilic drug dissolves in plasma easily and crosses membranes with difficulty. A hydrophobic drug crosses membranes easily and needs a carrier protein to travel in blood. You can predict a great deal about a molecule's route through the body from this one property.

Nursing

Whether a drug dissolves is why some of them need a certain fluid, a filter, or a line of their own.

Example: Oily preparations separate out or stick to tubing, so the route matters as much as the dose.

Radiologic technology

MRI is not really imaging tissue. It is imaging hydrogen, and nearly all the hydrogen in a person is sitting in water and fat.

Example: Two soft tissues look different on MRI because they hold different amounts of water and fat. That is why MRI separates soft tissues an X-ray cannot tell apart, and why a dense bone that dominates a radiograph can be nearly silent on MRI.

Medicine

Whether a molecule likes water or likes oil tells you where in the body it can get to.

Example: A fat soluble drug crosses into the brain readily. A water soluble one largely does not, which is why two similar drugs can have very different effects on alertness.

Respiratory therapy

An inhaled drug has to hold together as droplets the whole way down and still reach the small airways.

Example: Particle size and humidity change how much of a nebulized dose gets past the upper airway at all.

05

Solutions

Saying how much is in there

Mechanism

Molarity = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution

Units: mol/L, written M. Physiological concentrations are usually mM, millimoles per liter.

What it says: molarity counts particles, not mass. Equal masses of two different solutes are not equal numbers of molecules, and it is the number of molecules that determines the effect.

Equivalents = moles × charge per ion

Used for ions, because charge balance matters as much as particle count.

What it says: one mole of Ca2+ carries twice the charge of one mole of Na+. Body fluids must stay electrically neutral overall, so anions and cations move in matched sets.

Reading a lab value

  • Plasma sodium is reported around 140 mM. That is 140 millimoles in every liter of plasma.
  • Plasma glucose is often reported in mg/dL, a mass per volume, which is why it needs converting before it goes into any equation with molarity in it.
  • Whenever a value looks strange, check the units before you check the physiology.

Osmolarity and tonicity build on this and belong to Week 2, once the membrane is in place.

07

pH

Why the body cares so much

Mechanism

pH = −log[H+]

The log is the point. A drop of one pH unit is a tenfold rise in hydrogen ion concentration.

What it says: a pH change that looks tiny is not tiny. Going from 7.4 to 7.1 doubles the hydrogen ion concentration. That is enough to change the shape of proteins throughout the body.

  • Acid: releases H+ in solution. Base: accepts H+.
  • Buffer: a solution that resists a change in pH by binding or releasing H+ as needed.
  • The main buffer in blood is the bicarbonate system. You will build it properly in Week 13.

Predict before you look

A patient's arterial pH falls from 7.40 to 7.10. Nothing else about their body has changed. Name two things that must now be going wrong at the level of proteins, and say why each one follows from the pH change rather than from anything else.

Nursing

The first sign of an acid problem is often how the person is acting, not a number on a report.

Example: New confusion or agitation can arrive before anyone has drawn a gas.

Radiologic technology

The body holds its pH in a very narrow window, so anything you inject has to be mixed to match it.

Example: Older high-osmolar agents sat further from the body's own chemistry, and patients felt it as heat, pain and far more reactions. Modern low-osmolar agents are tolerated better because the chemistry is closer to what the blood already is.

Medicine

A pH change that looks tiny is not tiny. Every whole step on the scale is ten times more acid.

Example: Falling from 7.4 to 7.1 roughly doubles the hydrogen ion concentration, which is enough to change protein shape throughout the body.

Respiratory therapy

Reading acid and base status is the main skill of the entire specialty.

Example: Ventilation is the fast lever on pH, so a change in minute ventilation moves the pH within minutes, not hours.

09

Proteins

Shape is everything

Mechanism
  • Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
  • There are twenty amino acids. What differs between them is the side chain, and side chains are what make one region of a protein polar, another charged, another oily.
  • The sequence is set by the gene. Everything after that is folding, and folding is what makes the protein work.
  • Denaturation is loss of that shape, from heat, extreme pH, or certain chemicals. Function goes with it, and often it does not come back.

Nursing

Keeping a patient's temperature in range is really about protecting their proteins.

Example: Above roughly 40 C (104 F) the concern shifts from comfort to enzymes losing their shape.

Radiologic technology

MRI puts radio energy into the patient, and that energy turns into heat in the tissue.

Example: The SAR limit the scanner enforces on a sequence exists for this reason. It is a cap on how much the patient is allowed to warm, because tissue proteins have a temperature they will not survive.

Medicine

When a protein loses its folded shape it loses its job, and often it does not get that job back.

Example: That is why a very high body temperature is an emergency in its own right and not just an uncomfortable symptom.

Respiratory therapy

Heated humidifiers have a ceiling on purpose, because too hot burns the tissue you are trying to help.

Example: Delivered gas is warmed to body temperature and no higher, because airway proteins denature the same way any other protein does.

11

Binding

The five steps

Response
  • Ligand: any molecule that binds to a protein. A substrate is the ligand of an enzyme, a hormone is the ligand of a receptor.
  • Binding site: the pocket whose shape and charge distribution match the ligand.
  • Binding uses weak, reversible interactions, which is what allows the response to be switched off again.

Definitions

Specificity is how selective the site is about what it will accept. High specificity means it binds one ligand and refuses close relatives.

Affinity is how tightly it holds what it binds. High affinity means the complex stays together longer before it dissociates.

These are independent. A site can be fussy and hold loosely, or tolerant and hold hard.

Nursing

Reversal agents work by competing for the same site.

Example: Naloxone displaces opioid from the receptor, and if it wears off first the opioid can bind again and the patient re-sedates.

Radiologic technology

Gadolinium contrast is safe only because the metal is held inside a binding cage. The binding is the safety.

Example: Free gadolinium is toxic. It is injected locked into a chelate that holds it while it circulates and lets it be cleared intact. The entire concern about gadolinium retention is a question about how tightly that binding holds.

Medicine

Specificity is a property of the pair, not of the drug on its own.

Example: The same molecule produces different effects in different tissues, because the receptor it meets there is different.

Respiratory therapy

Receptor selectivity is why one bronchodilator is chosen over another.

Example: A drug aimed at airway receptors still reaches heart receptors, and that is where the tremor and the fast heart rate come from.

12

Binding

Saturation

Response
  • A protein has a finite number of binding sites, so the rate cannot rise forever.
  • Saturation is the point at which every site is occupied and adding more ligand changes nothing.
  • Simple diffusion never saturates. Anything that does saturate is telling you a protein is involved.

Predict

Glucose appears in the urine once plasma glucose passes roughly 180 mg/dL, and not before. Using saturation, explain why there is a threshold rather than a steady leak.

Nursing

The curve is steep in the middle and flat at the top. So the same size drop means something very different depending on where you started.

Example: A drop from 99 to 95 percent is small. The same four point drop from 90 to 86 is not.

Radiologic technology

Detectors fill up the same way proteins do. Past a point, more input buys you nothing.

Example: An overexposed region of a digital image is saturated: every pixel there is already reading maximum, so the detail is gone and more dose will not bring it back. It only adds exposure to the patient for no image.

Medicine

If something in the body saturates, a protein is behind it. And saturation gives you a cliff rather than a slow drift.

Example: Glucose appears in the urine only once the transporters are overwhelmed, which is why there is a threshold instead of a steady trickle.

Respiratory therapy

The oxygen and hemoglobin curve you will work with every day is exactly this shape.

Example: Above about 90 percent, adding more oxygen buys very little extra saturation, so the fix usually has to be somewhere other than the oxygen dial.

13

Modulation

Turning proteins up and down

Mechanism
  • Cofactor: a non-protein helper the protein needs in order to work at all. Often a metal ion.
  • Coenzyme: an organic cofactor, usually derived from a vitamin, which is one reason vitamin deficiencies produce such widespread effects.
  • Covalent modulation: attaching a group, most often a phosphate, which switches activity on or off. Phosphorylation is the body's main on-off switch.

Application

Methanol poisoning is treated with ethanol or fomepizole, which compete for the same enzyme that would otherwise turn methanol into formic acid. That works because the inhibition is competitive: flood the system with a rival substrate and you win.

An allosteric inhibitor cannot be beaten that way. More substrate does not help, because the active site itself has changed shape.

Nursing

A competitive blocker can be pushed off by more of the real thing, and that changes how a reversal behaves over time.

Example: As a reversal agent clears, the original drug can win the site back, so the patient has to be watched past the first improvement.

Radiologic technology

Premedication before contrast works by getting to the site first, which is why the timing is not negotiable.

Example: Diphenhydramine and steroids are given to occupy the sites histamine would use, ahead of the injection. A competitive blocker has to already be sitting on the receptor when the histamine arrives. Given afterward it is competing against a site that is already taken.

Medicine

Whether a blocker is competitive decides whether adding more of the real thing can beat it.

Example: Methanol poisoning is treated by flooding the same enzyme with a rival substrate, and that only works because the block is competitive.

Respiratory therapy

Some drugs bind somewhere else entirely, and no amount of the real thing will push them off.

Example: Adding more of the natural signal will not restore function if the active site itself has already changed shape.

14

Reactions

Law of mass action

Mechanism

A + B ⇌ AB

The double arrow is doing the work. Reversible reactions run both directions at once.

What it says: add more of anything on one side and the reaction shifts away from it. Take something away and it shifts toward that side to replace it. This single idea explains oxygen loading in the lung, carbon dioxide carriage in blood, and every buffer you will meet.

  • Reaction rate depends on concentration of reactants, temperature, and how much enzyme is present.
  • Enzymes speed a reaction by lowering the activation energy. They do not change the direction, and they are not consumed.

Perturb

Carbon dioxide combines with water to form carbonic acid, which dissociates into hydrogen ion and bicarbonate. A patient stops breathing adequately and carbon dioxide accumulates. Use mass action to predict what happens to their blood pH, and name every step in the chain.

Nursing

How fast someone breathes is a pH control, not only an oxygen control.

Example: A patient who stops breathing adequately becomes acidotic within minutes, well before any lab result returns.

Radiologic technology

The breath hold you coach on every chest CT is carbon dioxide piling up, and the patient is losing that race.

Example: Holding a breath lets carbon dioxide build, the reaction shifts toward acid, and that shift is what the patient feels as the urge to breathe. Coaching a few deep breaths first drives carbon dioxide down before the hold, which buys a longer and steadier hold and a sharper image.

Medicine

Push on one side of a reversible reaction and the whole thing slides the other way.

Example: Carbon dioxide accumulating shifts the reaction toward acid, and the pH falls as a direct consequence.

Respiratory therapy

Changing how much air someone moves each minute is the fastest way to change their blood pH.

Example: Change ventilation, carbon dioxide changes, and pH changes with it, all inside a few minutes.

15

Variants

Isozymes and proenzymes

Mechanism

Isozymes

  • Different forms of the same enzyme, catalyzing the same reaction with different efficiencies or in different tissues.
  • Because tissues carry different isozyme mixes, finding a particular form in blood tells you which tissue was damaged. That is the whole basis of cardiac enzyme testing.

Proenzymes

  • An inactive precursor, activated later by cutting off part of the molecule. Also called a zymogen.
  • Pepsinogen becomes pepsin only in the acid of the stomach. Digestive enzymes made in an active form would digest the organ that made them.
  • Acute pancreatitis is partly this control failing, with enzymes activating while still inside the pancreas.
