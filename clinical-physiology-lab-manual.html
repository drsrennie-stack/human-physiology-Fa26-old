Before you start

How this lab works

Read this once. It explains why the lab is built the way it is.

Why this lab is called clinical

The word clinical is doing real work in that title. This is not an attempt to turn a physiology class into a medical one. It is there because physiology turns into memorizable trivia the moment it stops touching anything real, and it stays learnable when it does.

Here is the honest reason.

Some of you are headed for provider roles. The level you land at decides how much of this you use directly. Some of you are headed for research, where you will meet the same measurements from the other side, as data you generate rather than data you act on.

But every single one of you, wherever you end up, is going to sit in a room while someone explains a result. It will be your own lab work. It will be your parents. Later it will be your children. Somebody in that room needs to understand what the number is actually measuring and what it would mean if it moved.

That person can be you.

That is also why the interpretation in this lab stops where it stops. You are not being asked to diagnose anyone. You are being asked to explain what the body is doing. That is both the more useful skill and the one that transfers everywhere.

The four skills underneath the content

Every week trains the same four skills. They matter more than any single test on the list, and they are the reason this is a lab instead of a reading assignment.

Measuring

Taking a real value off a real instrument, and knowing what the instrument physically detects. Every method has a blind spot, and knowing the blind spot is part of knowing the measurement. A dipstick pad detects a chemical group, not a cell. A pulse oximeter needs a pulse to work at all. A refractometer and an osmometer will give you two different answers about the same urine, and both are right for what they measure.

Calculating

Working the equations instead of being handed the output. When you have derived a number yourself, you know which inputs move it and by how much. That is what lets you reason about it later, in a room, under pressure, without the calculator.

Collecting data

Getting values in an organized, repeatable way with the conditions written down. Posture, timing, temperature, time of day, and how long a specimen sat all change results. A value without its conditions is not data yet.

Interpreting data, including charts

The one students skip most and need most. You will read curves, waveforms, tracings and tables all term, and you will be asked to say what mechanism produced the shape in front of you. Not what it is called. What caused it.

Reading charts is its own skill

Every week has one chart. For every chart in this lab, answer these four questions in order, out loud or on paper, before you say anything else about it.

  1. What are the axes, and what units are they in?
  2. What is the shape?
  3. What physically produces that shape?
  4. What would have to change to move it, and in which direction?

The charts you will meet fall into five kinds.

  • Curves whose shape means something. The oxygen dissociation curve. The ventricular pressure volume loop. The flow volume loop. Frank Starling. Enzyme kinetics. In each case you should be able to say what the axes are, what the slope represents, and what physically has to change to move the line.
  • Waveforms and tracings over time. The ECG. Capnography. The arterial pressure trace. The jugular venous waveform. Ventilator scalars. These read left to right as a sequence of events, so the skill is naming which event each deflection belongs to.
  • Titration and threshold plots. Glucose filtration, reabsorption and excretion against plasma concentration, with the transport maximum and the splay. Renal autoregulation across a pressure range. Dose response curves.
  • Panels and tables of numbers. A metabolic panel, a blood count, a blood gas, a urinalysis. These are charts too. The skill is reading them as a pattern rather than a list, noticing which values move together, and asking what single mechanism could produce all of them at once.
  • Trends against single values. One creatinine tells you very little. Three creatinines over three days tell you what is happening. A rising and falling troponin means something different from a flat elevated one. Direction and rate of change carry information no single point can.

Formulas you work by hand

MAP  =  DBP  +  SBP − DBP3

MAP mean arterial pressure SBP systolic, the peak DBP diastolic, the trough

In plain words

Your heart spends far more of each beat filling than ejecting, roughly two thirds of the time.

So the average pressure over the whole beat sits much closer to the diastolic number than halfway between the two. Start at diastolic and add back a third of the gap.

Below about 60 to 65, organs stop being able to defend their own blood flow.

PP  =  SBP − DBP

PP pulse pressure

In plain words

The size of the pressure swing each beat makes.

Two things widen it. A bigger stroke volume, or stiffer arteries that cannot absorb the push. In an older person a wide pulse pressure usually means stiff pipes, not a strong heart.

BMI  =  masskgheightmeters squared

BMI body mass index

In plain words

Weight compared against height squared, because bigger people are bigger in more than one direction.

It cannot tell muscle from fat. A very muscular person and a very heavy person can land on the same number, which is the main thing to know about it.

Formulas you work by hand

Osmest  =  2 × Na  +  glucose18  +  BUN2.8

Osm osmolality, mOsm/kg Na sodium, mEq/L 18 converts glucose from mg/dL to mOsm 2.8 converts urea nitrogen the same way

In plain words

Osmolality is just a headcount of dissolved particles.

Sodium is doubled because it always drags a negative partner along with it, so counting sodium twice counts the pair.

Glucose and urea are divided because they are reported in weight, not particle count, and those two numbers convert them.

Osmolal gap  =  measured − estimated

gap normally under about 10

In plain words

Work out what the osmolality should be from the particles you know about, then measure what it actually is.

If the real number is much higher, something is dissolved in there that you did not count.

v  =  Vmax × [S]Km + [S]

v reaction rate Vmax the ceiling rate, set by how much enzyme there is Km substrate level that gets you halfway to the ceiling, so a measure of grip [S] substrate concentration

In plain words

Add more substrate and the reaction speeds up, but only up to a point. Eventually every enzyme is busy and adding more changes nothing. That ceiling is Vmax.

Km is where you are at half the ceiling. A low Km means the enzyme grabs its substrate tightly. A high Km means it grabs loosely.

That is why the two numbers move independently. Vmax is about how many workers you have. Km is about how good each one is at catching.

Formulas you work by hand

Osmolarity  =  concentration × particles per molecule

particles NaCl gives 2, glucose gives 1

In plain words

Count particles, not molecules. Salt splits into two pieces when it dissolves, so it counts double. Sugar stays in one piece and counts once.

Tonicity  ≠  osmolarity

tonicity only counts particles that cannot cross

In plain words

This is the one that catches people out. Osmolarity counts everything dissolved. Tonicity only counts what is stuck outside the cell.

Urea crosses membranes freely, so it adds to osmolarity but does nothing to tonicity. A urea solution can be the same osmolarity as saline and still let a cell swell up and burst.

Formulas you work by hand

Eion  =  61z × log [ion]outside[ion]inside

E equilibrium potential in mV z the ion's charge, +1 for potassium, +2 for calcium 61 a constant that bundles temperature and the gas constant at body temperature

In plain words

This asks one question. If the membrane only let this one ion through, what voltage would build up before the ion stopped moving?

The ion moves down its concentration gradient, but it carries charge with it. That charge builds up and starts pushing back. The equilibrium potential is where the two forces balance and net movement stops.

Potassium sits around minus 90 mV, which is why resting membrane potential sits near there. The cell is mostly leaky to potassium at rest.

Corrected Ca  =  measured Ca  +  0.8 × (4 − albumin)

Ca total calcium, mg/dL albumin g/dL, normal about 4 0.8 how much calcium each gram of albumin carries

In plain words

About half your calcium is stuck to albumin and is not doing anything. Only the free half works.

So if albumin is low, the total looks low even though the working half is fine. This adds back the calcium that would have been carried if albumin were normal.

Measuring ionized calcium directly skips all of this, which is why it is preferred when it is available.

Conduction velocity  =  distancelatency

velocity m/s, normally 50 to 60 in large myelinated nerves

In plain words

Measure how far apart the two electrodes are, and how long the signal took to travel between them. Distance over time is speed.

Slow means the insulation is damaged. A small signal with normal speed means fewer fibers are left, but the survivors are working fine.

Plasma has to be electrically neutral. Every positive charge is matched by a negative one somewhere.

Of all the sodium the kidney filtered out of the blood, what share ended up in the urine?

Under 1 percent means the tubule is clinging to sodium hard, so the kidney thinks the body is short of volume. Over 2 percent means it is not clinging, so the tubule itself is not doing its job.

RSD how wide the spread of healthy values is 1.645 the step that cuts off the bottom 5 percent

In plain words

Healthy people scatter around the expected value, so normal is a range, not a number.

Step down 1.645 scatters and you have cut off the bottom 5 out of 100 healthy people. Those 5 are healthy. Below the line means unusual, not sick.

In plain words

Start with total air pressure. Subtract 47, because the airway soaks the air and water vapor takes up room.

Take the oxygen share of what is left. Then subtract a bit more, because carbon dioxide coming out of the blood also takes up space in there.

Formulas you work by hand

CrCl  =  UCr × VPCr

CrCl creatinine clearance, mL/min UCr urine creatinine concentration V urine flow rate, mL/min PCr plasma creatinine

In plain words

Clearance answers an odd question. Not how much creatinine was removed, but how many milliliters of plasma were completely cleaned of it per minute.

Work out how much came out in the urine, then ask how much plasma that must have come from. If plasma held 1 mg per 100 mL and 1 mg came out per minute, then 100 mL of plasma got cleaned.

That is why the answer is a volume per minute and not a mass per minute. It is a virtual volume, and it is the same idea for every clearance you will ever meet.

ACR  =  urine albuminurine creatinine

ACR albumin to creatinine ratio, mg/g

In plain words

A dilute sample makes everything look low and a concentrated one makes everything look high.

Creatinine is put out at a steady rate, so it tracks how concentrated the sample is. Dividing by it cancels the dilution out and means you do not need a 24 hour collection.
