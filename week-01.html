Before you start

What mastery means in this course

You choose how you learn it. What you have to be able to do at the end is the same for everyone.

You have not mastered something because you watched it, read it, highlighted it, or recognized it when you saw it. You have mastered a competency when you can retrieve what you know without your resources and use it to solve a problem you have not seen before.

Four levels, and only you can tell which one you are at

  1. I recognize it. I have seen this before. It looks familiar on the page.
  2. I can recall it. I can explain or reconstruct it without looking at anything.
  3. I can use it. I can apply it to a problem I have not seen before.
  4. I can reason with it. I can predict what happens when the system changes, and explain why.

In this course, mastery means the last two. Recall is the foundation you need before either is possible, but stopping at recall is what catches people out on an application question. Recognition is not on the list of things that will help you.

Every week has a Mastery Check built to tell you which level you actually reached. It carries no points and you can take it as many times as you want.

The mission

Maintain control

Why this week exists.

Your body is never sitting still. You walk into a cold room, you skip lunch, you climb a flight of stairs, and the conditions your cells live in start to drift. Cells only survive inside a narrow range, so something has to notice the drift and push back. Week 1 is about the machinery that does the pushing back, because every system you meet after this is a version of the same thing.

Hold onto the mission question while you work. You will come back to it in Apply, where you pick a real case and answer it for a specific patient.

By the end of this week, you should be able to

  • Explain homeostasis as something the body actively does, not a state it sits in.
  • Name every component of a control system and trace a variable through all of them.
  • Predict which direction a negative feedback response will push a variable, and say what makes the loop stop.
  • Use mass balance to say whether the amount of something in the body is rising, falling, or holding.
  • Read a physiological graph and say what it does and does not tell you.

03Practice

Use the physiology to make predictions.

Five items. Commit to an answer before you check, every time.

The routine is the same all semester: predict, commit, check, correct, explain. The commit step is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes the check worth anything. If you read the answer before you have chosen, you will recognize it and think you knew it.

1. You walk into a room at 10 °C (50 °F).

Your core temperature starts to drop below its set point. What happens to the radius of the blood vessels in your skin?

Predict, then commit
Choose an option first.

Narrower. Cold receptors in the skin report the drop, the hypothalamus compares it to the set point, and one of the effectors it reaches is the smooth muscle in the walls of skin arterioles. Those muscles contract, the vessel radius falls, and less warm blood is delivered to the surface where heat would be lost.

Notice the direction. The response opposes the change, which is what makes it negative feedback. Your fingers get cold precisely so your core does not.

If you chose wider: that is the response to being too hot, when the body is trying to dump heat at the surface. Same effector, opposite direction, and the direction is set by which way the variable moved away from the set point.

2. A patient has a fever. Their temperature is 39 °C (102.2 °F) and they are shivering under two blankets.

Shivering generates heat. Their temperature is already above normal. What does the shivering tell you?

Predict, then commit
Choose an option first.

The set point has been raised. Pyrogens act on the hypothalamus and move the set point up, say to 39.5 °C (103.1 °F). The loop is working perfectly. It compares 39 °C to the new set point, finds the body too cold, and turns on shivering and vasoconstriction to climb toward it. That is why a patient spiking a fever feels freezing.

When the fever breaks, the set point drops back to about 37 °C (98.6 °F), the same loop now finds the body too hot, and the patient sweats.

This is the single most useful idea in the week. The loop does not defend a number, it defends whatever the set point currently is. Change the set point and every response reverses without anything being broken.

3. Mass balance. A patient takes in 4.0 g of sodium a day and excretes 3.2 g a day.

Assume nothing else is producing or consuming sodium. What is happening to the amount of sodium in the body?

Predict, then commit
Choose an option first.

Rising, by 0.8 g a day. Mass balance says the amount in the body changes by intake plus production, minus output and minus metabolism. Here that is 4.0 minus 3.2, so 0.8 g a day accumulates. Over a week that is 5.6 g.

The third option is the interesting wrong answer. The kidney usually would compensate, and in a healthy person output would rise to match intake. But the numbers you were given say it has not. Mass balance is an accounting statement, and it does not care what should be happening.

Watch for this. When a question hands you numbers, work the numbers. Reaching for what normally happens instead of reading what you were given is how people lose marks on questions they understand.

4. Which of these stops on its own?

Think about what the response does to the original stimulus in each case.

Predict, then commit
Choose an option first.

Blood glucose. Insulin is released because glucose is high, insulin drives glucose into cells, glucose falls, and the fall is what shuts off insulin release. The response removes its own stimulus. That is negative feedback, and it is self-limiting by design.

The other two are positive feedback. Each platelet that sticks recruits more platelets; each contraction pushes the baby further down and triggers more oxytocin. Neither response removes its stimulus, it amplifies it, so neither can stop itself. Clotting ends when the vessel is sealed and labour ends when the baby is delivered. Both need an outside event.

The test to carry: ask what the response does to the stimulus. Removes it, negative. Increases it, positive. That question works on every loop in the course, and it is faster than trying to remember which examples go in which list.

5. A study reports that a patient's plasma sodium is 138 mmol/L.

The stated reference range is 135 to 145 mmol/L. Which of these does that single number let you conclude?

Predict, then commit
Choose an option first.

Inside the range, and nothing more. A concentration is an amount divided by a volume. A patient who has lost sodium and water together can hold a perfectly normal concentration while their total body sodium is well down, and a patient who is retaining both can do the same in the other direction.

You also cannot call the regulation normal from one value. A single point tells you nothing about a trend, and a system working extremely hard to hold a value looks identical, on one measurement, to a system with nothing to do.

This is the habit Investigate is built around. Ask what the number actually measures, then ask what you would need to know before you could act on it. Usually the answer is a second measurement.

05Investigate

What does the evidence show?

This week's lab: where "normal" actually comes from.

Every reference range you will use for the rest of the course was built by measuring a lot of people and drawing a line somewhere. This lab is about where that line comes from, what it means when a patient falls outside it, and why "outside the range" and "sick" are not the same statement.

The reasoning structure, and you will use it every week

  1. Question. What are you actually trying to find out?
  2. Prediction. Write it before you look at any data, and write why.
  3. Evidence. What the measurements say, graphed with both axes labeled and units on both.
  4. Interpretation. What the evidence supports, and what it does not.
  5. Conclusion. Your answer to the question, and what would have changed your mind.

What to do

  1. Open the reference range lab and work it by hand, in your two pen colors.
  2. Plot the data it gives you. Label both axes with units and mark the reference range on the graph itself.
  3. Answer every question, including the clinical reasoning ones at the end.
  4. Photograph or scan your pages into one PDF, in order, and submit it in Canvas.

There is no PhysioEx this week. PhysioEx starts in Week 2.

Open the reference range lab How to submit it
If your graph is not coming out right
  • The independent variable, the thing you changed or the thing time did, goes on the x axis. The thing you measured goes on the y axis.
  • Every axis gets a label and a unit. A y axis reading "sodium" is not finished. It reads "plasma sodium, mmol/L".
  • Mark the reference range as a band or a pair of lines, not as a note off to the side. The point of graphing it is to see where the patient sits relative to it.
  • The math section of the notes covers units, conversions, and reading a slope.

06Apply

Choose a case. Use the physiology.

Four cases, four different rooms, the same physiology underneath. Pick one.

All four cases are about a body holding a variable steady while something pushes on it. They differ in who the patient is and what you are looking at, not in how hard they are. Pick the one closest to where you are heading, or pick the one furthest from it, whichever you would rather spend an hour on.

Whichever case you choose, answer these five

  1. Name the physiological variable being regulated, and the control system doing the regulating.
  2. Predict what is happening to that variable, and in which direction.
  3. Explain the mechanism, component by component, all the way through the loop.
  4. Interpret the evidence you were given. Say what it shows and what it cannot show.
  5. Justify your conclusion, and name the one measurement that would change it.

Case A  |  Nursing

She stood up and went gray

Your patient has been in bed for three days. Sitting, their blood pressure is 118/74 with a heart rate of 78. You stand them up and after one minute it is 96/60 with a heart rate of 104. They say the room went dark for a second.

Work it: what is being defended, what did gravity do to it, and what does the heart rate tell you that the blood pressure alone does not? Then say whether this loop is failing or working hard.

Case B  |  Medicine

The fever that keeps climbing

A patient arrives at 38.2 °C (100.8 °F), shivering and asking for a blanket. Two hours later they are 39.6 °C (103.3 °F), have stopped shivering, and are sweating heavily. Nothing was given between the two readings.

Work it: the set point moved twice. Say when and in which direction each time, and explain how the same unchanged loop produced two opposite responses.

Case C  |  Radiology

Three numbers after the scan

A patient has a contrast enhanced CT. Their serum creatinine is 0.9 mg/dL the day before, 1.4 mg/dL at 48 hours, and 1.0 mg/dL at day seven. The reference range is 0.6 to 1.2 mg/dL.

Work it: only one value is outside the range, so say what the trend shows that any single value could not. Then say what the return tells you about whether the control system was damaged or loaded.

Case D  |  Exercise and allied health

Sixty minutes in the heat

A runner exercises for an hour at 32 °C (89.6 °F). Core temperature rises from 37.0 °C (98.6 °F) to 38.6 °C (101.5 °F) over the first thirty minutes, then holds there for the second thirty despite the same workload.

Work it: explain the rise, then explain the plateau, which is the harder half. Say what is happening at minute 45 that was not happening at minute 5, and what would break the plateau.

What a strong answer looks like

Length is not what makes it strong. A good answer to any of these fits on one page and does four things:

  • It names the variable precisely. Not "blood pressure" but "mean arterial pressure", not "temperature" but "core temperature", and it says why that distinction matters here.
  • It traces the loop in order rather than jumping from stimulus to outcome. Every component gets named, including the ones that are easy to skip.
  • It separates what the evidence shows from what you are inferring. Both are allowed. Confusing them is not.
  • It names what would change the answer. The single most useful sentence in a clinical write up starts "I would want to know".

Bring your answer to the reflection discussion. You will be asked which case you chose and why.

One post. It carries the physiology and your thinking about it.
