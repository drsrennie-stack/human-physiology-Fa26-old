      What a strong answer looks like

      Length is not what makes it strong. A good answer fits on about a page and does four things.

      • It names the thing precisely. Not "her sodium is low" but which sodium, measured or corrected, and what each one is a concentration of.
      • It traces the mechanism in order rather than jumping from cause to outcome. Every step gets named, including the easy ones to skip.
      • It separates what the chart shows from what you are inferring. Both are allowed. Confusing them is not.
      • It names what would change the answer. The most useful sentence in a clinical write up starts "I would want to know".

      A correct number with no explanation earns half credit. That is true every week, on every question.

      Using AI on this one

      Open, with disclosure. Use It is the category where AI is allowed, and where you have to say what you used it for.

      • Use it to check a definition, ask how a mechanism works, or push back on your own reasoning.
      • At the end, add a line or two: what you used it for and what you did with what it gave you. "I asked it to explain the corrected sodium and then rewrote question three because I had the direction backwards" is a good disclosure.
      • What is graded is whether your naming, your mechanism and your interpretation hold together as one person thinking, week over week, about one patient. That is very visible when it is missing.

      Undisclosed use is the only version of this that is a problem.
