What did the evidence tell you, and what did you do with it?

Reflect · the metacognitive discussion

Post due Friday at 10:00 pm · Replies due Sunday at 10:00 pm

This is not a study skills exercise and it is not a feelings check. It asks for the same three moves you make in a clinical write up, turned on yourself: decision, evidence, adjustment.

Here is the logic. This week you chose how to learn this material. Your Mastery Check then gave you evidence about whether that choice worked, and it gave it to you before any grade depended on it. What you do with that evidence is the single most useful skill this course can hand you, and it is the one thing nobody can do for you. So we make it visible, once a week, in writing.

Answer these four

  1. What did your Mastery Check reveal? Name one specific thing you thought you understood and the check showed you had not mastered yet. Be precise. "Feedback loops" is not an answer. "I could name the three parts of a control loop but I kept calling the integrating center a receptor" is an answer.
  2. What did you do about it? Did you change the resource, change the approach, practice it differently, draw it, say it out loud, ask someone? Or did you look at it and decide what you were already doing was working? That last one is a legitimate answer if you can say how you knew.
  3. What happened when you tried again? Say whether it held the second time, and how you could tell. "It felt better" is not evidence. "I could rebuild the loop from a blank page without looking" is evidence.
  4. The physiology one.

Only the fourth question changes from week to week. The first three stay all fifteen weeks, on purpose. The point is to watch what you do with evidence about your own learning, and to watch that change over a term.

One rule, and it is not negotiable

Do not post your Mastery Check result. Not the number, not how many you got, not how many attempts it took.

Nobody learns anything from that and it turns the thread into a leaderboard. The Mastery Check carries no points precisely so that you can be honest in it. Posting scores would undo that in a week. What belongs here is the decision you made and what the evidence told you afterward.

The same goes for your practice exams. If you want to say something about how they are going, say whether you are improving a lot, improving a little, holding steady, or sliding. That is the useful part and it is all anybody needs. Numbers and percentages are optional, and nobody should feel any pressure to post one.

How long should this be?

Shorter than you think. A strong post is usually four short paragraphs, one per question, and fits on a single screen. Length is not what makes it good. Specificity is. One named gap, one named change, one honest report on whether the change worked, beats a page of general reflection every time.

What a reply that counts looks like

Reply to at least two classmates. A reply that counts does one of these:

  • Takes the adjustment they made and asks what would happen if they tried it on something else.
  • Names a place their reasoning and yours came apart, and asks about it rather than correcting it.
  • Offers what worked for you on the same gap, specifically enough that they could actually do it tomorrow.
  • Asks the question their post left open. Often the most useful reply in the thread.

"Great post, I struggled with that too" is kind, and it is not a reply that counts. Add the sentence that comes after it.

Post it

  1. Post your four answers in this week's Reflect discussion in Canvas. Take your Mastery Check first, since three of the four questions are about what it showed you.
  2. Reply to at least two classmates by Sunday at 10:00 pm. Reading how other people are studying, and what is not working for them, is most of the value of the thread.
  3. Text is fine. If you would rather talk than type, a two minute video of you answering the four questions counts as your post.

If this week did not go well

Say so, plainly, and answer the four questions anyway. A post that says "I ran out of time, I did the videos and skipped the retrieval, and the Mastery Check showed me exactly what that cost" is a strong post. It is honest, it names the decision, and it reads the evidence.

Nothing in this discussion is graded on whether the week went well. It is graded on whether you looked at what happened and said something true about it.
