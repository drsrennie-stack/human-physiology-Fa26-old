What this week covers

Getting across the membrane. Work through these in order. Each line is one thing you will be able to do by Sunday.

  1. Body fluid compartments. State the approximate volumes of total body water, intracellular fluid, extracellular fluid, plasma, and interstitial fluid in a 70 kg adult and compare the dominant solutes of the intracellular and extracellular compartments.
  2. Compartment separation and clinical volume shifts. Predict the direction of water movement between compartments when extracellular osmolarity rises or falls and name a clinical situation that produces each shift.
  3. Membrane composition and fluidity. Describe the fluid mosaic membrane and state how phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids, and integral and peripheral proteins contribute to its properties.
  4. Determinants of permeability. Rank molecules by their ability to cross a lipid bilayer unaided using size, charge, and lipid solubility, and predict which will require a protein.
  5. Simple diffusion and Fick's law. State the variables in Fick's law of diffusion and predict how a change in concentration gradient, surface area, membrane thickness, or distance changes the rate of transfer.
  6. Osmolarity and tonicity. Calculate the osmolarity of a solution, distinguish osmolarity from tonicity, and classify a solution as isotonic, hypotonic, or hypertonic to a cell.
  7. Osmosis and cell volume. Predict the direction of water movement and the resulting change in cell volume when a cell is placed in a solution of stated osmolarity and penetrating solute content.
  8. Diffusion and osmosis experiment. Measure diffusion and osmotic movement across a selectively permeable membrane and relate the observed rate to molecular size and concentration gradient.
  9. Tonicity and red blood cells. Observe erythrocytes in solutions of different tonicity, identify crenation, normal shape, and hemolysis, and explain each result.
  10. Facilitated diffusion. Describe carrier and channel mediated diffusion and explain why carrier mediated transport shows saturation and specificity while simple diffusion does not.
  11. Primary active transport. Explain how the sodium potassium ATPase uses ATP to move three sodium out and two potassium in, and state the two gradients it maintains.
  12. Secondary active transport. Distinguish symport from antiport and trace how the sodium gradient powers glucose uptake by SGLT and calcium removal by the sodium calcium exchanger.
  13. Transport maximum and saturation. Interpret a transport rate curve, identify the transport maximum, and apply the concept to renal glucose handling in hyperglycemia.
  14. Vesicular transport. Compare phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor mediated endocytosis, and exocytosis by trigger, cargo, and energy requirement.
  15. Transepithelial transport. Trace glucose or sodium from lumen to blood across a polarized epithelium and identify which step occurs at the apical membrane and which at the basolateral membrane.
  16. Transport simulation. Use a membrane transport simulation to distinguish simple diffusion from facilitated diffusion and active transport by their response to gradient reversal and metabolic poison.
