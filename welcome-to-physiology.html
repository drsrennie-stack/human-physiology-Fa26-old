Welcome to Human Physiology.

This course is organized one week at a time. You do not need to figure everything out today.

What this course is about

One idea, used everywhere.

Anatomy asks what the parts are. Physiology asks how they work, and what happens when they stop. Over fifteen weeks you will build one idea and then use it in every system in the body: your body is constantly being pushed off balance, and almost everything it does is the work of pushing back.

By December you should be able to look at a patient, a lab value or a graph and say what is being regulated, which direction the body is pushing, and what would happen if one step in that chain failed.

What you will learn

Three parts, fifteen weeks.

Part 1 · Weeks 1 to 3

How cells hold the line

Homeostasis, feedback loops, and the chemistry that does work in the body. How things get across a membrane, and why holding a difference costs energy.

Part 2 · Weeks 4 to 8

Signals, movement and control

The electrical signal and how a nerve fires, synapses, the senses, how muscle makes force, and the hormones that run the slower half of control, including reproduction.

Part 3 · Weeks 9 to 15

The systems that keep you alive

The heart and circulation, blood and immune defense, digestion, breathing and gas transport, the kidney, and how the body defends its pH from both the fast end and the slow one.

A little about how I teach

Rigorous and flexible at the same time. Those are usually treated as a trade. They are not.

The flexibility is real. Nothing in this course meets at a set time. You choose how you learn each week: watch it, read it, draw it, work problems, or say it out loud to an empty room. You choose which application case you solve, so the physiology lands in the room you are actually heading for, whether that is nursing, medicine, radiology, physical therapy or something else. You choose what to change when something is not working. I have no interest in dictating how you learn, because you know things about your own life and your own head that I do not.

The rigor is also real. What you have to be able to do at the end is the same for everyone, and it is not recognizing an answer on a list. It is retrieving what you know with nothing open and using it on a problem you have not seen before. You will be asked to prove that, out loud, on video, by drawing a mechanism and teaching it to me.

So: choice in how you learn, one common standard for what you can do. Every week gives you a Mastery Check that carries no points and tells you honestly whether the way you chose actually worked, while there is still time to change it.

What I expect, and what you can expect from me

I expect you to be accountable for demonstrating mastery, not for performing effort. Turning things in on time is not the same as learning, and I will not pretend it is.

In return: everything is written down, the weeks all look the same so you are never hunting, no deadline is a surprise, and when you ask a question in the virtual office you will get a real answer that also helps everyone else in the class.
