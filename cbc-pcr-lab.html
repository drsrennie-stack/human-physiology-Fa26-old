A CBC is a census, not a diagnosis

Every number on a complete blood count is answering one of three questions. How many cells are there, how big are they, and what are they doing. Nothing on the report tells you what is wrong with the patient. It tells you how the bone marrow, the spleen, the kidneys and the immune system are currently behaving, and you have to reason backward from that behavior to a cause.

That backward reasoning is the whole skill, and it is worth saying plainly why we are practicing it this way. If I hand you a case and ask what is wrong, you can get a confident answer out of an AI in four seconds and learn nothing. So in this lab you commit to a mechanism in writing before anything unlocks, you build a case that has to survive a consistency check, and you finish with a drawing that has to hold up when you explain it out loud.

The three questions

  • How many. RBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white count, platelet count. These are population counts, and they move when production, destruction or dilution changes.
  • How big and how full. MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW. These describe the average red cell and how uniform the population is, which points straight at what the marrow was missing while it built them.
  • What they are doing. The differential and the reticulocyte count. This is the functional read. It tells you which arm of defense is mobilized and whether the marrow is answering the call.

Four habits that make the pattern show up

  1. Scan by line, not by number. Red line, white line, platelet line. Is one line off, two lines, or all three. That single observation cuts the possibilities more than any other move you can make.
  2. Size before cause. On an anemia, go to MCV first. Small, normal or large sends you down three completely different branches.
  3. Ask whether the marrow is answering. The reticulocyte count separates a marrow that cannot make cells from a marrow that is making them as fast as it can while something destroys them.
  4. Check the numbers against each other. The indices are calculated from each other, so they have to agree. When they do not, the problem is the sample or the report, not the patient.

      Section 4

      What PCR actually does

      A CBC tells you how the host is responding. It almost never tells you what the host is responding to. Neutrophils rise for strep, for a ruptured appendix and for a bad burn, and the blood count cannot tell those apart. To name the organism you need a test that looks for the organism itself, and the polymerase chain reaction is the one that finds it fastest and in the smallest amounts.

      PCR copies one specific stretch of DNA over and over until there is enough of it to detect. The specificity comes from the primers, which are short single strands of DNA built to match sequences that flank the target. If the organism's genetic material is in the sample, the primers find it and copying begins. If it is not, nothing amplifies.

      The cycle, three steps, about thirty to forty times

      1. Denature, near 95 degrees Celsius. Heat breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the double helix together and the strands separate. Nothing is destroyed, because hydrogen bonds are what heat can undo without touching the sugar phosphate backbone.
      2. Anneal, roughly 50 to 65 degrees. Cooling lets the primers bind to their matching sequences. This temperature is the specificity control. Too low and primers stick to sequences that only roughly match, which is where false positives come from.
      3. Extend, near 72 degrees. A heat stable DNA polymerase builds the complementary strand from free nucleotides. The polymerase has to survive repeated trips to 95 degrees, which is why it comes from bacteria that live in hot springs.

      Each cycle doubles the target. Thirty cycles from a single copy is more than a billion copies, which is why PCR can find an organism long before the patient's own antibodies would show up on a blood test.

      Two variations you have to know

      RT-PCR. Influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2, hepatitis C and HIV carry RNA, not DNA, and polymerase cannot copy RNA. So the assay starts with reverse transcriptase, which builds a DNA copy of the RNA, and the normal cycling runs from there. When you see RT-PCR on a report, the organism was an RNA organism.

      Quantitative PCR and the Ct value. A fluorescent signal rises as copies accumulate, and the cycle at which it crosses the detection threshold is the Ct. More starting material means the threshold is crossed sooner, so a low Ct means a high organism load and a high Ct means very little. Ct is not a unit of illness severity, it is a measure of how much target was in that particular swab, and swab quality alone can move it several cycles.

      Multiplex panels. Several primer sets run in one reaction, so a single respiratory or gastrointestinal panel reports twenty or more organisms at once. Convenient, and it creates the interpretation problem below.

      What a positive PCR does and does not prove

      It does tell you

      • Genetic material from that organism was present in that specimen.
      • Roughly how much was there, if the assay is quantitative.
      • Sometimes a specific gene, such as a resistance marker, which changes treatment without waiting for culture.

      It does not tell you

      • That the organism is alive. PCR amplifies nucleic acid from dead organisms just as happily, which is why a test can stay positive for weeks after someone recovers.
      • That the organism is causing the illness. Plenty of organisms are carried without disease, and a positive result on a multiplex panel can be a bystander.
      • That the organism is absent when the test is negative. A swab that missed, a specimen taken too early, or an organism that lives somewhere other than where you sampled all produce a negative.

      This is the reason your case needs both halves. The CBC shows the host response, PCR names a candidate, and only the two together with the story make an argument. A case built on a positive PCR alone can be defeated by a classmate pointing out that the patient could simply be a carrier.

      PCR

      Finds the organism's nucleic acid. Fast, hours. Extremely sensitive. Cannot tell living from dead, and gives no drug susceptibility beyond the specific resistance genes it targets.

      Culture

      Grows the organism. Slower, one to several days, and less sensitive after antibiotics have started. Proves viability and gives full susceptibility testing.

      Serology

      Measures the patient's antibody response. Needs time for antibodies to develop, so it is poor for early illness, and it can stay positive long after exposure. Useful when the organism is hard to sample directly.

      Check yourself before you build

      A patient finished treatment three weeks ago and feels well. A repeat PCR is still positive. What happened?

      Residual nucleic acid from organisms that are no longer viable is still in the specimen, and PCR amplifies it regardless. This is why a test of cure is usually done by culture or by clinical response rather than by repeating a PCR.

      A respiratory panel returns positive for two viruses. The patient has one illness. How do you argue which one matters?

      You go back to the host response and the timeline. Ct values give a rough sense of which organism is present in greater quantity, the differential pattern and the symptom onset have to line up with the organism you are claiming, and one of the two may simply be shedding left over from a recent illness. The argument comes from the whole picture, not the panel.

      Two patients, same organism, Ct of 18 and Ct of 34. Which one is sicker?

      You cannot say. The one with a Ct of 18 had far more target in that specimen, but sampling technique, how many days into the illness the swab was taken, and the site sampled all move Ct independently of how ill the person is. Ct is a property of the specimen before it is a property of the patient.

      Why can PCR detect an infection days before serology can?

      PCR looks for the organism, which is present from the moment of infection. Serology looks for the patient's antibody, which takes days to a couple of weeks to become detectable because B cells have to be activated, class switch and proliferate first. Early in illness the organism is there and the antibody is not.

      Build the case backward

      Solving a case and building one are not the same skill, and the second is harder. To construct a patient whose numbers hang together you have to understand causation running forward, from organism to host response to the specific value printed on the report, and you have to keep every number consistent with every other number.

      You will be assigned an organism. Your job is to work out what it does to a person, what the blood count would look like while that is happening, which PCR test would actually be ordered and what it would show, and then write the case so that someone else can solve it without being told the answer.

        Section 6

        The flow chart, drawn by hand

        After you have worked the charts, you know something you cannot easily say out loud, which is the order in which you actually check things. The flow chart is where that order becomes visible. You are not drawing a summary of blood disorders. You are drawing the decision path you personally used, in the sequence you used it, with the questions written the way you asked them.

        It has to be drawn by hand on paper, in pen or pencil, and photographed. Paper only. No tablet, no stylus, no drawing app, no diagramming software, no AI generated chart. Laying out the branches yourself, on a surface where you cannot undo and rearrange, is the moment the material gets organized in your head. Cross outs and arrows squeezed into the margin are welcome. They are evidence of thinking, not sloppiness.

        Your path audit from section 2 gets submitted alongside the drawing, and the two have to agree. That pairing is the whole integrity design, and it is worth understanding rather than working around.

        Where AI helps here, and where it cannot follow

        You are expected to use AI in this lab. It is genuinely good at some of this and genuinely unable to do the rest, and knowing the line is part of what you are learning.

        Use it for

        • Asking why a mechanism works the way it does after you have committed to your own read.
        • Arguing the other side. Have it defend the option you rejected as strongly as it can, then decide whether it beat you.
        • Rehearsing your explanation and telling you where it was unclear.
        • Attacking your built case. Ask it to find the contradiction. If it finds one, fix it.

        It cannot produce

        • Your path audit. That is a record of what you did, chart by chart, and no other source generates it.
        • A drawing in your hand on paper.
        • An account of what changed in your own reasoning, which is the graded half of every chart.
        • Your seeded organism, since no two students have the same one and no published answer exists.
        • A live unscripted walkthrough of a patient moving through your chart.

        Try this and watch what happens. Take chart 13 and paste those numbers into any AI tool, asking what condition the patient has. It will very likely hand you a confident diagnosis, because the model is agreeable and the numbers look like numbers. Then run the same values through the index checker in section 3. You are required to include that exchange in your log with the contradiction you found, and it is the clearest demonstration in this course of why a tool that answers everything cannot be trusted to notice when a question is broken.

        Required decision points

        Your chart has to branch on all six of these somewhere, in whatever order you found yourself using them.

        1. Do the numbers agree with each other. This one goes first for a reason, and one of the thirteen charts will show you why.
        2. How many lines are affected. One line, two lines, or all three.
        3. Cell size. Low, normal or high MCV, with RDW read alongside it.
        4. Is the marrow responding. Reticulocyte count, high or low.
        5. Which white cell is elevated. Neutrophils with or without bands, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, or immature forms that should not be in circulation at all.
        6. Platelet involvement. Isolated, or part of a wider failure.

        Requirements

        • Hand drawn on paper, photographed, legible at phone screen size. Test it before you post.
        • Every branch is a question with a yes or no, or a small set of exclusive answers. No branch may be a topic heading.
        • At least eight of the thirteen chart patterns end somewhere on your chart, and you label those endpoints.
        • At least one endpoint says stop and recheck the specimen rather than naming a disease.
        • Somewhere on the chart, mark the point where a CBC alone stops being able to answer and a PCR or another test has to be ordered.
        • Sign it with your name.

        The discussion

        1. Post your chart. Photo of the drawing, plus a Canvas Studio video of three minutes or less. Walk one of the thirteen patients through your chart out loud, from the first question to the endpoint. Do not summarize what the chart contains, drive a patient through it.
        2. Post your built case. Presenting note and CBC only. Do not name your organism and do not post your PCR result yet.
        3. Reply to two classmates. Run their posted case through your own flow chart and say where you land. Then say where their chart would struggle, using a specific patient it cannot resolve. Be concrete and be kind, the point is to find the gap, not to score on anyone.
        4. Close your own thread. Reveal your organism and PCR result, say whether your classmates landed where you intended, and describe one change you would make to your flow chart based on what came back.

        Every chart in this class breaks somewhere. Finding out where yours breaks, in public, with a real case, is the part that makes the next version good. Nobody loses points for a chart that turns out to have a gap. Points come off for a chart nobody could break because it never committed to anything.
