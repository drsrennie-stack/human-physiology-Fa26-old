Part 1. The cell, the plasma membrane, and how cells connect to each other.

Week 2. Getting across the membrane.

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie

1

Slide 1 · Chapter 3, page 61

The cell is the basic unit of structure and function

Everything your body does, it does because cells do it. Whenever you follow a whole body process far enough back, the explanation ends up inside a cell.

1

A cell is the smallest unit of life

It is the smallest thing that can do all of what we call living: take in fuel, release energy, respond to its surroundings, and reproduce.

2

All organisms are made of one or more cells

Some organisms are a single cell doing every job. You are somewhere near thirty trillion of them, each doing one small part.

3

Cells arise from other cells

New cells are only ever produced by the division of cells that already exist. Nothing in your body assembles a cell from scratch.

An organism maintains homeostasis through the coordination of all its cells, working individually and together.

Robert Hooke first saw and named cells in 1665, looking at a slice of cork through a microscope he built himself. The three statements above were assembled by several scientists over the two centuries that followed.

2

Slide 2 · Figure 3.1

About 250 kinds of cell, built from four elements

Human cells differ enormously in shape, size and lifespan. The shape is almost always a clue to the job, so when a cell looks unusual, ask what that shape lets it do.

Cell diversity

~250

Distinct human cell types have been identified. A red blood cell, a neuron and a fat cell look nothing alike because they do nothing alike.

Composition

C H N O

Carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and oxygen make up almost all of a cell’s mass. Oxygen alone is about 65 percent of your body weight.

Compare and contrast

Somatic cells

Body cells. Every cell that is not an egg or a sperm. They carry 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, divide by mitosis, and produce copies of themselves.

Compare and contrast

Sex cells, or germ cells

Eggs and sperm, and the cells that give rise to them. A mature gamete carries 23 chromosomes, is produced by meiosis, and combines with its partner at fertilization to restore 46.

Somatic cells are diploid and divide by mitosis. Gametes are haploid and are produced by meiosis.

3

Slide 3 · Figure 3.2

Most human cells have three basic parts

Part 1

Plasma membrane

The outer boundary. It separates the fluid inside the cell from the fluid outside it, and it controls what crosses in either direction.

Part 2

Cytoplasm

Everything between the membrane and the nucleus. It is cytosol, the fluid itself, plus the organelles suspended in it.

Part 3

Nucleus

The control center. It holds the DNA and directs which proteins the cell builds.

Not every cell follows the model. A mature red blood cell has no nucleus at all, and a skeletal muscle fiber has many.

3b

Slide 3 continued · Extracellular materials

Cells sit in fluid, not in empty space

A cell is never on its own. It is surrounded by fluid it did not make, solutes it must sort through, and a meshwork that holds it in place.

ECF

Extracellular fluid

The fluid outside cells. Interstitial fluid sits between the cells, blood plasma stays inside vessels, and cerebrospinal fluid surrounds the brain and spinal cord.

Solutes

Dissolved solutes

Nutrients on the way in, wastes on the way out, ions, and chemical signals traveling to whichever cells carry the matching receptor.

Secretions

Cell secretions

Products cells release to work outside themselves. Saliva, mucus and gastric juice from exocrine glands, and serous fluid that keeps membranes sliding past each other.

ECM

Extracellular matrix

A jellylike meshwork of proteins and polysaccharides that fills the spaces and helps glue neighboring cells together.

Everything a cell needs arrives through the extracellular fluid, and everything it makes leaves the same way. That is why membrane transport is the next thing we study.

Slide 4 · Focus Figure 3-1, pages 64 to 65

The fluid mosaic model

The membrane is not a fixed wall. It is a two layer sheet of lipid, 7 to 10 nanometers thick, with proteins drifting through it. Fluid describes the movement, mosaic describes the mixture of pieces set into it.

Cholesterol, about 20 percent of membrane lipid Glycolipids, about 5 percent Integral proteins, most of them transmembrane Peripheral proteins

Polar heads face the water on both sides. Nonpolar tails face each other through the middle. Cholesterol sits between the tails and keeps the sheet from getting too loose or too stiff.

4b

Slide 4 continued · Primary functions

What the plasma membrane actually does

1

Selectively permeable boundary

It separates intracellular fluid from extracellular fluid. Some substances cross freely, some cross only with help, and some do not cross at all. Selective is the important word.

2

Regulates exchange and transport

Water and solutes move across under control, which is the only reason the inside of a cell can have a different composition from the fluid around it.

3

Senses the outside environment

Receptors in the membrane read chemical messengers and changes in the composition of the extracellular fluid, then pass the message inward.

4

Provides structural support

Junctions built into the membrane hold cells to their neighbors and to the matrix, so a tissue can be pulled on without coming apart.

If a membrane loses selective permeability, the cell swells or shrinks to match whatever surrounds it. That is why choosing an IV fluid is a physiology decision and not a routine.

5

Slide 5 · Recall from Chapter 2

Phospholipids are amphipathic, and that is why the bilayer builds itself

Each phospholipid has a polar head that water is attracted to and two nonpolar tails that water is not. One molecule, two opposite personalities. That is what amphipathic means.

Head

Polar and hydrophilic

The head is polar, so water is attracted to it and surrounds it. Heads therefore face outward on both faces of the membrane, toward the extracellular fluid on one side and toward the cytosol on the other.

Tails

Nonpolar and hydrophobic

The tails are nonpolar, so water is not attracted to them and pushes them aside. They turn inward and hide against each other, forming an oily core through the middle of the sheet.

Nobody assembles the membrane. Drop phospholipids into water and they arrange themselves this way, which is also why a small tear reseals on its own.

6

Slide 6 · Figure 3.3

Membrane proteins: transporters and receptors

Proteins are about half the mass of the membrane and carry out most of its specialized work. Integral proteins are embedded in the bilayer itself, and most of them run all the way through, which is why they are called transmembrane proteins.

Transporter

Channel

A water filled pore through the protein. Solutes move through it down their gradient. Some channels stand open, others are gated and open only on a signal.

Transporter

Carrier

Binds the solute on one side, changes shape, and releases it on the other. Because there are only so many binding sites, a carrier can be saturated.

Transporter

Pump

A carrier that spends ATP to move a solute against its gradient, uphill. The sodium potassium ATPase is the one to know.

Receiver

Receptor

Binds one specific ligand, a chemical messenger or an ion. Binding changes the protein’s shape, and that change starts a response inside the cell.

A hormone travels everywhere in the blood but only affects cells carrying its receptor. The message is broadcast, the receptor decides who listens.

7

Slide 7 · More protein jobs

Enzymes, cell markers, and peripheral proteins

Function

Enzymes

Some membrane proteins are enzymes that catalyze reactions right at the surface, so the reaction happens exactly where the product is needed. Lactase sitting in the lining of the small intestine splits lactose as it arrives.

Function

Cell markers in the glycocalyx

Glycoproteins and glycolipids carry sugar chains on the outer surface. Together they form the glycocalyx, a sticky surface that helps anchor adjoining cells and provides biological markers for cell to cell recognition.

Location

Peripheral proteins

Not embedded in the bilayer. They rest loosely on integral proteins or on the lipid heads. On the inner face they anchor the membrane to the cytoskeleton and give the cell its shape. On the outer face they help hold the cell to its neighbors. Some are enzymes.

Your glycocalyx is how your immune system tells your own cells from anything else. Blood typing before a transfusion and tissue matching before a transplant both come down to reading this sugar coat.

8

Slide 8 · Cell adhesion molecules

CAMs: the proteins that make a tissue a tissue

Cell adhesion molecules are transmembrane glycoproteins that stick cells to each other or to the extracellular matrix. They are also the handholds a cell uses when it needs to move.

1

They hold tissues together

A CAM anchors its cell to the neighboring cells and to the matrix, so the tissue keeps its arrangement while it is stretched, squeezed or twisted.

2

They guide movement

A migrating cell, such as a fibroblast crawling into a healing wound, grips CAMs ahead of it and pulls itself forward, then releases behind.

3

They signal

Many CAMs pass a message inward once they bind, telling the cell where it is, what it is touching, and what it should do next.

A white blood cell finds an infection using CAMs. Inflamed vessel lining displays more of them, so a passing white cell slows, rolls, sticks, and then squeezes between the endothelial cells at the right place.

9

Slide 9 · Figure 3.4

Membrane junctions: the three jobs cells need done

Occluding

Seal it

One structure does this. The tight junction fuses neighboring membranes in a belt right around the cell and closes the space between them, so anything crossing the sheet has to go through the cells rather than between them. The blood brain barrier and the lining of the bladder work this way.

Anchoring

Hold it

Three structures do this, and they are not interchangeable. Adherens junctions form a belt tied to actin. Desmosomes are spot welds tied to intermediate filaments. Hemidesmosomes bind the cell downward to its basal lamina. Next slide takes these apart.

Communicating

Talk through it

One structure does this. A connexon, built from connexin proteins, lines up with a connexon in the next cell to open a tunnel between the two cytoplasms. Ions and small molecules pass straight through, which is how cardiac muscle cells share an electrical signal and contract as a unit.

Tight junctions seal the space between cells. The three anchoring junctions hold cells in place. Gap junctions let ions and small molecules pass from one cell to the next.

9b

Slide 9 continued · The anchoring family

Three anchors, and only two of them are related

In an epithelial cell these sit in a fixed order from the apical surface down, which is worth learning as a sequence rather than as three separate facts.

Belt

Adherens junction

A continuous band just below the tight junction. Cadherins reach across the gap and tie, on the inside, to a bundle of actin running parallel to the membrane. Because actin contracts, this belt lets a whole sheet of cells change shape together.

Spot

Desmosome

A rivet rather than a belt. Cadherins again, but tied to intermediate filaments, the rope like ones built to resist pulling. Those run deep into the cell, so the load spreads across the cytoskeleton. Skin and cardiac muscle are full of them.

Half, but not really

Hemidesmosome

Looks like half a desmosome, built from different molecules. Integrins, not cadherins, binding downward to laminin in the basal lamina instead of sideways to another cell.

In pemphigus vulgaris the antibodies attack desmosomes, so the skin layers separate from each other and blister. In bullous pemphigoid they attack hemidesmosomes, so the whole epidermis lifts off the basement membrane.

Slide 10 · Figure 3.2, Table 3.4

The cytoplasm is a workspace, not a bag of fluid

Cytoplasm is everything between the plasma membrane and the nucleus. It is crowded, organized, and divided into compartments, and the compartments are the whole point.

Part 1

Cytosol

The fluid itself. Mostly water, with dissolved proteins, salts and sugars. It behaves as a solution for the small solutes and as a colloid for the large proteins, which is why it can shift between watery and gel like.

Part 2

Organelles

The working parts, each one a compartment with a job. Most are wrapped in their own membrane so the chemistry inside them can differ from the cytosol around them.

Part 3

Inclusions

Stored material rather than machinery. Glycogen granules in liver and muscle, lipid droplets in fat cells, melanin granules in skin. Inclusions come and go with supply and demand.

A membrane around a space lets a cell run reactions that would interfere with each other if they happened in the same fluid. Digestive enzymes and the proteins they would destroy exist in the same cell only because they are kept in separate compartments.

11

Slide 11 · Non-membranous organelles

Organelles with no membrane around them

These sit directly in the cytosol, so their work happens in the open. Three groups to know.

1

Cytoskeleton

Three filament families, from thinnest to thickest. Microfilaments of actin sit under the membrane and handle shape and movement. Intermediate filaments are rope like and resist pulling, which is why they anchor into desmosomes. Microtubules are hollow tubes of tubulin that act as the cell’s tracks and build the mitotic spindle.

2

Centrioles

Paired barrels of microtubules near the nucleus. They organize the spindle during division and form the base of every cilium and flagellum.

3

Ribosomes

The sites of protein synthesis. Free ribosomes in the cytosol build proteins the cell will use itself. Ribosomes bound to rough ER build proteins headed for a membrane, a lysosome, or export.

The cytoskeleton is not scaffolding that sits still. It is taken apart and rebuilt constantly, which is how a cell changes shape, divides, and crawls.

12

Slide 12 · Membranous organelles

Organelles wrapped in membrane

Synthesis

Rough ER

Studded with ribosomes. It builds and folds proteins headed for export, for membranes, or for lysosomes, and it makes the membrane phospholipid itself.

Synthesis

Smooth ER

No ribosomes. Lipid and steroid synthesis, detoxification of drugs and alcohol, and calcium storage. In a muscle cell it is the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the calcium store that runs contraction.

Sorting

Golgi apparatus

Receiving, tagging and shipping. It takes what the ER makes, modifies it, and packages it into vesicles addressed to the membrane, to a lysosome, or out of the cell.

Energy

Mitochondria

Where most ATP is made. Double membrane, their own DNA, inherited from your mother. A cell’s mitochondrial count tracks its energy demand, which is why cardiac muscle is packed with them and a fat cell is not.

Breakdown

Lysosomes and peroxisomes

Lysosomes hold acidic digestive enzymes that break down worn out organelles, bacteria and debris. Peroxisomes handle free radicals and detoxification, using catalase to convert hydrogen peroxide into water.

A liver cell is the clearest example. It is packed with smooth ER for detoxification and with peroxisomes for free radicals, which is why the liver is where alcohol and most drugs are broken down.

13

Slide 13 · Figures 3.23 and 3.24

Specializations of the plasma membrane

Three extensions of the membrane itself. Two of them move things, one of them just adds surface. Tell them apart by asking what moves: the fluid, or the cell.

Absorption

Microvilli

Finger like extensions supported by actin. They multiply the surface area available for absorption. In the small intestine they form the brush border, and the enzymes of the brush border sit right on them.

Movement of fluid

Cilia

Longer and slender, with a microtubule core and motor proteins that make them beat rhythmically in one direction. They sweep material across a surface. Respiratory tract cilia move mucus, reproductive tract cilia move gametes.

Movement of the cell

Flagella

Same internal machinery, much longer, one per cell, and it propels the cell rather than the fluid. In humans the only flagellated cell is sperm.

Smoke first paralyzes cilia and then destroys them, so mucus stops being swept out of the airway. The smoker’s cough is doing by force the job the cilia used to do quietly.
