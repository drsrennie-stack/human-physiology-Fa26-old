Reading numbers

Four kinds of number

Before you interpret any value, decide which of these four you are holding.

Kind one

An amount in the body

Total body water, 42 L in a 70 kg adult. The iron in your stores. Glycogen in liver and muscle. Total body potassium. The mineral in bone.

These are quantities and they can run down. In iron deficiency the amount is the problem, and it is corrected by replacing iron. Ferritin reports a stock, not a rate.

Kind two

A concentration, an amount per volume

Sodium 140 mEq/L is an amount divided by a volume. It can move because the sodium moved or because the water moved, and the number alone does not identify which.

That is why the compartments are taught before the panel is read.

Kind three

A rate, per unit time

Glomerular filtration rate, cardiac output, ventilation, clearance, enzyme activity. Each of them is an amount per minute.

An enzyme assay measures product per minute. One reading at the end is not a rate. A rate needs two points and the time between them.

Kind four

A reserve, what an organ can still do

Vital capacity, ejection fraction, transport maximum, the filtration remaining after nephrons are lost.

This is how organ failure is measured, and it behaves more like an amount than a rate. Everyday numbers stay normal while the reserve is being spent, and they move only once it is gone.

4

Reading numbers

A steady concentration means two rates are equal

90 mg/dL

What holds a fasting glucose at 90 mg/dL

Glucose enters the blood from the liver and the gut and leaves it into cells continuously. A reading of 90 mg/dL is the concentration at which those two rates are equal.

The same reading an hour later usually means both rates have changed together, not that nothing moved.

1.2 mmol/L

What holds a resting lactate at 1.2 mmol/L

Production by glycolysis is matched by clearance, mostly in the liver. Change either side and the number moves.

The concentration alone does not identify which side changed. That is why a raised lactate is a question rather than a diagnosis.

5

Reading numbers

Three things can move a concentration

A concentration is an amount divided by a volume, held steady by rates on both sides.

An amount can fall for weeks while the concentration reads normal, because the body defends the concentration first. Total body iron and total body potassium both behave this way.

  • The amount of solute in the body changed.
  • The volume of water it is dissolved in changed.
  • A rate on one side of the balance changed.

Water and solution properties

Water is a polar molecule

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons harder than hydrogen does, so the oxygen end of a water molecule carries a slight negative charge and the hydrogen ends carry a slight positive charge. That separation of charge is what polar means.

Because of it, water molecules attract one another. Those weak attractions are hydrogen bonds, and each water molecule can make up to four of them at once.

Water and solution properties

What dissolves in water, and what does not

Polar and charged solutes dissolve because they join the hydrogen bond network. Nonpolar solutes cannot join it, so they are excluded and end up clustered together.

Water is not repelling them. Water molecules hydrogen bond more favorably with each other than with a nonpolar molecule, so the nonpolar molecules are left pressed together. That exclusion is what assembles a membrane in Week 2.

Water and solution properties

A hydrogen bond between two water molecules

Each hydrogen bond is weak and lasts only picoseconds. There are enough of them that water is liquid at 37 C, and without them a molecule this small would be a gas.

9

Water and solution properties

Four solutes, hydrophilic or hydrophobic

A solute dissolves in water if it carries a charge or can hydrogen bond.

Na+

A sodium ion in plasma

Hydrophilic. It carries a full positive charge, so water molecules turn their negative oxygen ends toward it and surround it in a shell. It dissolves freely and travels in plasma unassisted.

This is why sodium sets extracellular osmolality. It is dissolved, all of it, all the time.

Glucose

Glucose at 90 mg/dL

Hydrophilic. It carries hydroxyl groups, each of which hydrogen bonds with water, so it dissolves easily.

The same property is a limitation. A water soluble molecule cannot cross a lipid membrane on its own, so glucose needs a transport protein. That is Week 2.

Cortisol

A steroid hormone in blood

Hydrophobic. Steroids are built from a fused ring skeleton with almost nothing polar on it, so water will not take it up.

It travels in blood bound to a carrier protein, crosses the membrane directly, and binds a receptor inside the cell. Slower in onset, longer in duration. Week 9.

Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen gas

Hydrophobic, and only slightly soluble. Two identical atoms share their electrons evenly, so there is no charge separation for water to hold onto.

That is why hemoglobin is needed. Plasma alone carries almost no oxygen, which is the subject of Week 12.

10

Water and solution properties

Three consequences of the same water property

Why a membrane forms without anything holding it together

A phospholipid has a polar head and two nonpolar tails. In water the heads join the hydrogen bond network and the tails are excluded from it.

The lowest energy arrangement available is a double layer with the tails inside and the heads facing water on both sides. Nothing holds them there. That arrangement costs the least energy. Week 2.

Why a fat soluble hormone acts slowly and lasts for hours

It cannot dissolve in plasma, so it travels bound to a carrier protein. It then crosses the membrane and binds a receptor inside the cell.

A receptor inside the cell changes which genes are transcribed, which takes time and then persists. A water soluble hormone binds a surface receptor instead: faster onset, shorter duration. Week 9.

Why oxygen crosses a membrane and glucose cannot

The interior of the membrane is nonpolar, so nonpolar molecules pass through it and polar molecules do not.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross directly. Glucose, amino acids and every ion require a protein to carry them. The membrane is selective because its interior is lipid, not because it selects.

Counting what is dissolved

Osmolality counts dissolved particles per kilogram of water

Normal serum osmolality is 275 to 295 mOsm/kg, and the body defends it to within about one percent. Three values on an ordinary chemistry panel predict most of it.

estimated osmolality = 2 x Na+ + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8

Na+ in mEq/L. Glucose and blood urea nitrogen in mg/dL. The 18 and the 2.8 convert weight into particle count.

Sodium is doubled because each sodium ion is accompanied by an anion, usually chloride or bicarbonate, so doubling the sodium counts the pair.

Glucose and urea are reported by weight, and osmolality is a particle count, so each is divided by the factor that converts one into the other.

Counting what is dissolved

The osmolal gap is measured minus estimated

Osmolal gap = measured osmolality minus estimated osmolality. A normal gap is under about 10.

A large gap means the plasma contains osmotically active particles that the three measured solutes do not account for. The gap does not name the substance, and it is still worth reporting before the substance is known.

pH and buffers

pH measures free hydrogen ion on a log scale

pH is the negative logarithm of the free hydrogen ion concentration. Pure water at pH 7 has an H+ concentration of 0.0000001 mol/L, and 7 is easier to write.

One pH unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A lower pH means more hydrogen ion and a more acidic solution.

pH and buffers

The range arterial blood is held in

Arterial blood is held between 7.35 and 7.45. Below 7.35 the blood is acidic, above 7.45 it is alkaline. Outside roughly 6.8 to 7.8 survival is brief.

The lungs and the kidneys defend that range continuously, and Week 14 is the full account of how.

pH and buffers

Why the pH range is so narrow

Hydrogen ions bind the charged side chains of a protein and change its shape. A protein with the wrong shape does the wrong job or no job at all. Every enzyme, channel and receptor in this course is a shape that pH can alter.

20

pH and buffers

What goes wrong outside the range

In each case hydrogen ions change how a protein behaves.

pH 7.15

Why strongly acidic blood lowers blood pressure

Extra hydrogen ions bind the contractile and receptor proteins of vascular smooth muscle and cardiac muscle and alter their shape.

Vessels respond less to noradrenaline and the heart contracts less forcefully. The catecholamines are still present. The receptors respond less. That is a shape problem, not a signaling problem.

pH 7.60

Why hyperventilation causes tingling and cramps

Blowing off carbon dioxide raises pH. With fewer hydrogen ions competing for the negatively charged sites on albumin, more calcium binds to albumin instead of remaining free.

Total calcium is unchanged. Ionized calcium falls, and ionized calcium is the fraction nerve and muscle use, which produces the tingling and the spasm.

K+ 6.1

Why acidic blood raises plasma potassium

Hydrogen ions move into cells to be buffered there, and potassium moves out to maintain charge balance. Cells exchange ions rather than containing them.

A patient with an acid problem often arrives with a potassium problem attached. Correct the acid and the potassium moves back. This returns in Week 4.

pH and buffers

A buffer is a weak acid with its conjugate base

Add acid and the base half takes up hydrogen ions. Add base and the acid half releases them. The pH still moves, but far less than it would without the pair.

CO2 + H2O <-> H2CO3 <-> H+ + HCO3-

The bicarbonate pair. Normal arterial values: PCO2 35 to 45 mmHg, HCO3- 22 to 26 mEq/L, pH 7.35 to 7.45.

A buffer is a weak acid and its conjugate base in the same solution. Read the reaction in both directions, because it runs in both.

Add acid from the right and the reaction runs left: H+ joins a bicarbonate, becomes carbonic acid, then carbon dioxide and water, and the hydrogen ion has left solution.

22

pH and buffers

Two organs control the two halves of the pair

Minutes

How the lungs set the carbon dioxide half

Ventilation determines how much carbon dioxide is blown off, so the lungs move PCO2 within minutes. Chemoreceptors sense the change in pH and adjust the rate and depth of breathing.

This is the fast control, and it is why a metabolic acid load is followed within minutes by deeper breathing.

Hours to days

How the kidneys set the bicarbonate half

The kidney reclaims filtered bicarbonate and generates new bicarbonate, over hours to days. That is the slow control.

Because the two halves are set independently by two organs on two timescales, blood pH is a whole body problem. One knob can be failing while the other compensates.

You will do this in lab At the bench you add the same acid to a buffered tube and an unbuffered tube and record both meters.

Protein structure and function

Four levels of protein structure

1

Primary, the amino acid sequence

The order of the amino acids, written by the gene. That is all it is, a sequence.

Change one amino acid and every level above it can change. Sickle cell disease is one substitution in the beta globin chain, and the result is a hemoglobin that polymerises and a red cell that deforms.

2

Secondary, local hydrogen bonding along the backbone

Alpha helices and beta sheets, formed by hydrogen bonds between parts of the backbone that lie near each other in the chain.

These are local shapes. They are the units the protein folds up out of, not the finished structure.

3

Tertiary, the folded shape that makes the pocket

The whole chain folds into one compact three dimensional shape, driven largely by hydrophobic amino acids being excluded from water into the interior. That is the same exclusion of nonpolar groups from water described earlier.

This is the level that produces function. The fold brings amino acids that were far apart in the sequence next to each other, and the pocket they form is the active site or the receptor site.

4

Quaternary, several folded chains assembled

Two or more finished chains joining into one functional unit. Hemoglobin is four chains.

This is what makes cooperation possible, because one subunit binding oxygen changes the shape of its neighbors. That is why the oxygen curve is sigmoid rather than linear. Week 12.

You will do this in lab In the Week 1 lab your enzyme is a tertiary structure with a pocket in it, and every condition you change acts on that pocket.

Protein structure and function

What heat and pH do to protein shape

Denaturation is the loss of a protein's three dimensional shape, and it is often permanent.

About 41 C

Why a fever above 41 C is an emergency

Heat is molecular motion. Above roughly 41 C the motion is enough to break the weak interactions that hold a protein's fold together, and enzymes unfold across every tissue at once.

They do not refold when the patient is cooled. That is why heat stroke is treated as time critical and why the treatment is immediate cooling.

pH 6.9

Why enzymes work badly at pH 6.9

Not by unfolding. Hydrogen ions load onto the charged side chains of a protein and change how tightly it binds, so enzymes work badly at pH 6.9. Blood proteins do not come apart there. That change of charge is reversible, which is why patients are brought back from an arterial pH near 6.8 with the same proteins they went down with.

What makes the last stretch accelerate is mechanical. The liver clears lactate more slowly as the pH falls, phosphofructokinase is inhibited so glycolysis stalls, and the heart contracts less forcefully so delivery drops. Less clearance and less delivery, so acid arrives faster than it leaves. That loop unwinds when the cause is treated.

The kitchen

Why a fried egg never turns clear again

Egg white is mostly ovalbumin, transparent because the folded protein is small and soluble. Heat unfolds it, the buried hydrophobic parts reach the surface, and they stick to each other.

The result is an opaque solid, and cooling does not restore the clear liquid. Ovalbumin is not the protein your lab reports at 3.5 to 5.0 g/dL, and it is not from your species. It is the same class of molecule under the same rules, which is the only reason a frying pan illustrates anything about serum.

You will do this in lab In your Week 1 assay the rate climbs, then falls sharply, and the tube that fell does not recover when you cool it.

Protein structure and function

The two protein numbers on a panel

3.5 to 5.0 g/dL

Serum albumin, and why it appears on almost every panel

It is the main plasma protein, made only by the liver, and it generates most of the colloid osmotic pressure that holds water inside the blood vessel.

Low albumin shifts Starling forces toward filtration, so fluid leaves the vessels and collects in tissue. That is the swelling. Its half life is about 20 days, so it reports on weeks rather than hours.

6.0 to 8.3 g/dL

Serum total protein, and why it means little alone

It is albumin plus every globulin, summed. Two independent pools with different sources, added into one number.

A normal total protein can therefore contain a low albumin next to a high globulin. Read total protein together with the albumin, or you are reading a sum and calling it a measurement.

Read total protein with the albumin beside it. The sum alone does not say which pool changed.

Enzyme activity and regulation

What an enzyme does

An enzyme lowers the activation energy of a reaction, the energy barrier that has to be crossed before the reaction proceeds. Lowering it means a far greater fraction of the molecules have enough energy to react at any moment, so the reaction goes faster.

It does this by holding the substrates in a pocket, in the right orientation, in a chemical environment that stabilises the transition state. That pocket is the tertiary fold.

On the course list 18. Enzyme activity and regulation

Enzyme activity and regulation

What an enzyme cannot do

  • It cannot change the products. The reaction still makes what it was going to make.
  • It cannot make an impossible reaction happen. If the energetics forbid the reaction, an enzyme changes nothing.
  • It is not consumed. One enzyme molecule turns over substrate repeatedly, thousands of times a second in some cases.
  • Inhibition slows an enzyme. It does not destroy it.

An enzyme changes how fast a reaction goes, and nothing else about it.

Enzyme activity and regulation

Two constants describe an enzyme

Adding substrate raises the rate until every enzyme molecule is occupied. That flattening is saturation, and this equation is its arithmetic.

v = (Vmax x [S]) / (Km + [S])

v is the reaction rate. Vmax is the ceiling rate. Km is the substrate concentration that gives half the ceiling rate. [S] is substrate concentration.

Vmax is set by the amount of enzyme present. Double the enzyme and the ceiling doubles.

Km is a measure of binding. A low Km means the enzyme binds substrate tightly and reaches half maximal rate at a low substrate concentration. A high Km means it binds loosely and needs more substrate.

Set [S] equal to Km and the equation gives v = Vmax x Km divided by 2Km, which is half of Vmax. That is the definition of Km, produced by the algebra.

Work it with me

The same enzyme with a non competitive inhibitor

Work it with me3 min

A non competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere and alters the shape. Vmax falls from 100 to 60 micromol/min, Km stays 5 mmol/L.

Enzyme activity and regulation

Two drugs, and the mechanism each one uses

The dosing of each drug follows from the mechanism.

Statins

Why a statin is taken every day

A statin is a competitive inhibitor of HMG CoA reductase, the rate limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis. Its shape resembles the natural substrate closely enough to occupy the same pocket.

Competitive inhibition is reversible and can be overcome by substrate, so the effect lasts as long as the drug concentration does. Stop the drug and the enzyme is unchanged and returns to work.

Fomepizole

Why antifreeze poisoning is treated by blocking an enzyme

Ethylene glycol itself is not the toxin. Alcohol dehydrogenase converts it into acids that damage the kidney, so the metabolite is the poison.

Fomepizole is a competitive inhibitor that occupies alcohol dehydrogenase with far higher affinity than ethylene glycol has. The conversion nearly stops and the parent compound is excreted in the urine unchanged. Block the reaction and the kidney does the removing.

Enzyme activity and regulation

Temperature and pH change rate by different mechanisms

Both curves rise to a peak and fall. What happens on the way down differs, and so does whether the enzyme recovers.

Temperature

Why the rate climbs and then falls sharply

On the way up, heat is more molecular motion, so substrate and enzyme collide more often and more collisions carry enough energy. That part is gradual and reversible.

Just past the optimum, near 37 C for most human enzymes, the fall is modest and largely reversible: at 45 C your own run loses about a fifth of its rate and much of that returns on cooling. Further up the fold gives way permanently. By 55 C there is essentially nothing left and cooling that tube restores nothing. That far end is denaturation.

pH

Why an enzyme has a pH optimum

The active site works because specific side chains carry specific charges. Changing the pH adds or removes hydrogen ions from those side chains, and the pocket loses its grip on the substrate.

Mild shifts are reversible: return the pH and the rate returns. Extreme shifts unfold the protein and it does not. Pepsin in the stomach peaks near pH 2, pancreatic enzymes in the small intestine near pH 8.

You will do this in lab In the Week 1 lab you run one of these two variables, plot rate against it, and mark the optimum on your graph. Write the predicted shape down first.

Four rules for measuring a rate

1

Measure per unit time, not a total

An assay measures how fast product appears, or how fast substrate disappears, per unit time. Absorbance change per minute. One reading at the end of ten minutes is a total, and a total cannot show when the reaction slowed.

Read at fixed intervals and take the slope of the early, straight part of the line. That is the rate.

2

Change one variable at a time

Substrate, or temperature, or pH. Hold everything else constant.

Changing temperature and pH in the same run produces a number that cannot be interpreted. Change one thing at a time or you cannot say what caused what.

3

Include a tube with no enzyme

The control tube contains everything except the enzyme. It measures how much of the reaction proceeds without the enzyme.

Subtract that from every other reading. What remains is what the enzyme did, which is what you set out to measure.

4

Predict the shape at high temperature

The rate climbs, peaks, then falls sharply as the enzyme denatures. Write that prediction down before you run it.

That drop is the result, not a technique error. Reported as an error, it discards the most informative point on the graph.

You will do this in lab This is your Week 1 lab build project. You write a tool that simulates an enzyme assay and analyses it, reporting interpretation and clinical relevance rather than a number alone. Design it yourself and log every AI exchange.

ATP and energy coupling

Three kinds of work that ATP pays for

Hydrolysis of ATP releases energy, and the cell couples that release to a reaction that would not proceed on its own. The two reactions are joined on the same protein.

Transport

Moving an ion against its gradient

Movement against a concentration gradient does not happen on its own. The sodium potassium pump is the standard example: three Na+ out and two K+ in per ATP, both against their gradients.

That pump runs in every cell continuously and uses a substantial share of resting energy expenditure. Everything electrical in Week 3 is paid for by it.

Mechanical

Shortening a muscle

Physical movement. In the crossbridge cycle myosin binds actin, pulls, and then requires an ATP to release.

That is the mechanism of rigor mortis. ATP is required for the release step, not the pulling step, so when ATP runs out the muscle locks rather than relaxing. Week 5.

Chemical

Building a large molecule

Assembling a large molecule from small ones costs energy each time: linking amino acids into a protein, glucose into glycogen, or synthesizing a neurotransmitter.

The body is rebuilt continuously rather than once. Every one of those bonds is paid for with ATP, which is why building tissue costs calories and why starvation consumes muscle.

ATP and energy coupling

The size of the ATP pool and the flow through it

Your body contains roughly 250 g of ATP at any moment, about half a cup. ATP weighs about 507 g per mole, so that is close to half a mole.

Over a day at rest you turn over close to your own body weight in ATP. For a 70 kg adult that is about 140 moles a day, which is about a tenth of a mole every minute.

ATP and energy coupling

What the pool size means for interpretation

Half a mole in the pool, a tenth of a mole leaving every minute: the pool would be gone in about five minutes if nothing replaced it. In hard exercise turnover rises several fold and the pool does not get bigger.

  • The amount of ATP present says little about a cell's energy state.
  • The rate of ATP production against the rate of demand says most of it.
  • The clinical question is whether supply is keeping up with demand.

Real laboratory tests

Serum glucose against hemoglobin A1c

Two tests, one variable, two different windows of time.

70 to 99 mg/dL

Serum glucose

The window this number reports on

This second. It is the free glucose concentration in plasma at the moment the needle went in, and it is a regulated variable, defended by insulin on one side and by glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone on the other.

It moves with the last meal, with stress, and with a night of poor sleep. A single glucose is a snapshot of a controlled variable, and controlled variables move most.

4 to 5.6 percent

Hemoglobin A1c

The window this number reports on

The last two to three months. Glucose attaches to hemoglobin slowly and non enzymatically, and once attached it stays for the life of the red cell. A1c is the fraction that became glycated.

It is not regulated. Nothing defends it and nothing corrects it. It is a cumulative chemical record, the integral of glucose exposure rather than its current value.

About 120 days

Red cell lifespan

How A1c moves when glucose has not

A1c measures exposure time, and exposure time is set by how long red cells live. Red cells normally last about 120 days.

Shorten that lifespan and cells are replaced before they accumulate glycation, so A1c reads falsely low in a patient whose glucose has not changed by a single mg/dL. Hemolysis does this, as do recent transfusion, blood loss, and iron replacement producing a burst of new red cells.

Read together

The rule

What to do when the two disagree

Ask what would make each of them wrong, and check whether that condition is present in this patient.

A high glucose with a normal A1c indicates something recent. A normal glucose with a high A1c indicates that the last three months were worse than today. A disagreement between them is information, not an error.

Before reading any laboratory value, establish what period of time it reports on.

Real laboratory tests

Lactate rises when glycolysis outruns the mitochondria

Resting lactate is 0.5 to 2.2 mmol/L. Lactate is the end product of anaerobic glycolysis, and it accumulates when glycolysis runs faster than the mitochondria can take up pyruvate and oxidize it.

A raised lactate therefore reports a mismatch between two rates, and identifying which rate changed is the clinical task.

50

Real laboratory tests

Three reasons lactate rises

Lactate often rises before blood pressure falls, because perfusion is inadequate before the cuff reading changes.

  • Delivery: the pump or the circulating volume is failing, so blood is not arriving.
  • Use: blood is arriving but the mitochondria cannot use the oxygen in it.
  • Clearance: the liver is not recycling lactate, so normal production accumulates.

A raised lactate does not name a cause. Establish which of the three is present.
