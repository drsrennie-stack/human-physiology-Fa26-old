What this Unit covers

Three questions, in this order

1

What the membrane is made of

A double sheet of phospholipid with cholesterol wedged between the tails, sugar chains on the outer face, and proteins sitting in it or on it. That description is called the fluid mosaic.

Structure comes first because every rule that follows is a consequence of it.

2

What crosses without a protein

Three properties decide it: whether the molecule carries a charge, whether it dissolves in fat, and how large it is. Those three properties rank any molecule.

Fick's law then puts a rate on the crossing, and the rate is applied to a lung.

3

Which way the water moves

Water crosses almost every membrane and it follows solute. The question to answer is always which solute is trapped on which side.

That question is tonicity, and the second half of this deck answers it.

3

The case

Kofi M. is losing water through his gut

Kofi M. is 24. In Week 1 you measured the volume he lost and followed his body defending its blood pressure. That work was done in liters and compartments, at the level of the whole body.

A toxin holds a chloride channel open in the lining of his gut. Chloride pours into the lumen and water follows the salt out of him. Nothing pumped the water. It moved because the solute moved.

4

The case

Oral rehydration runs the same physics in reverse

Oral rehydration solution contains salt and sugar together because one transporter in the gut wall carries sodium and glucose in on the same trip. Where the sodium goes, water follows.

Neither half of that story can be followed until you can say what the membrane is and what crosses it. That is this deck.

Membrane structure

The three lipid components

Each one does something the other two cannot.

Phospholipid

Why a bilayer assembles on its own

Each phospholipid is amphipathic: a phosphate head that dissolves in water and two fatty acid tails that do not. In water the tails turn to face each other, and the resulting double sheet is the lowest energy arrangement available. Nothing assembles it.

The same arrangement reseals after a puncture, which is why a cell survives an injection and why membranes can bud off as vesicles.

Cholesterol

What holds fluidity steady as temperature moves

Cholesterol wedges between the fatty acid tails and buffers fluidity in both directions. Warm, it obstructs the tails and stiffens the sheet. Cold, it keeps the tails from packing tight and freezing solid.

A red cell membrane holds close to one cholesterol for every phospholipid, and all of it is doing that one job. What lets a red cell fold through a capillary is a different structure, the spectrin mesh and the spare surface area.

Glycolipid

Why sugar chains face outward only

Sugars are how one cell identifies itself to another. They are added on the outer face and never the inner one, so the two faces of a membrane are not interchangeable.

The ABO blood group is exactly this. A and B are sugars added to the ends of chains on the red cell surface, and O is the unfinished chain. A transfusion reaction is an immune system reading a sugar it does not recognize.

Membrane structure

Where the proteins sit, and what fluid means

Integral protein

What requires a protein spanning the whole membrane

Anything that connects the outside of the cell to the inside. A pore for an ion, a carrier that binds on one face and releases on the other, a pump, a receptor that senses outside and signals inside.

They sit in the membrane because a stretch of their amino acids is nonpolar and is stable in the oily core. Removing one requires breaking the membrane.

Peripheral protein

What the inner face does without entering the oil

Holding shape. Peripheral proteins clip onto the inner surface and onto each other, forming a mesh that gives the cell its form and keeps the membrane from tearing when it is stretched.

In a red cell that mesh is spectrin and ankyrin. Damage it and the cell rounds up into a sphere with no spare membrane, and a sphere with no spare membrane bursts in a solution a normal cell tolerates.

The word fluid

How far the parts of a membrane move

A single phospholipid exchanges places with its neighbor millions of times a second and can travel the length of a bacterium in about one second. Proteins drift as well, far more slowly, because they are much larger.

The mosaic is therefore not a fixed pattern. It is a crowd, and the arrangement is different every second.

Membrane proteins

Three proteins that move a solute

About half the mass of the membrane is protein, and each of these does a job the lipid cannot.

Channel

How an ion crosses a million times a second

A channel is a water filled pore through an integral protein. Once it is open the ion moves down its gradient with nothing to slow it. Channels are therefore fast, and they cannot move anything uphill.

Most channels are gated, so the cell controls when the pore exists. The cholera toxin in Kofi's gut holds one chloride channel permanently open.

Carrier

How glucose crosses, being large and polar

A carrier binds the molecule on one face, changes shape, and releases it on the other. It is far slower than a channel because it moves on every cycle, and it saturates, because a cell has only so many carriers.

Carriers are covered in the transport deck this week, including what saturation does in the kidney.

Pump

How a solute is moved against its gradient

A pump is a carrier that hydrolyzes ATP, and it is the only protein that spends ATP directly to move a solute.

There is a second route uphill. The transport deck this week covers carriers that spend no ATP of their own and still move cargo against its gradient, by riding a gradient the pump already paid for.

The sodium potassium pump is the one to know. Potassium sits at 140 mM inside and sodium at 145 mM outside, and that mirror image is the reason a membrane voltage exists.

Membrane proteins

Three proteins that move no solute

Receptor

How insulin gives an order without entering the cell

A receptor spans the membrane, senses on the outside and signals on the inside. The message crosses without the messenger crossing, which is the solution to being a peptide hormone in a body full of lipid films.

Insulin acts within minutes rather than seconds. The receptor phosphorylates itself within seconds of binding, but moving GLUT4 carriers into the membrane and dropping the glucose takes five to fifteen minutes. A steroid takes hours.

Enzyme

Why an enzyme is anchored on the outer face

So that it can act on a molecule before that molecule is transported. Enzymes anchored on the outer surface cut large molecules into pieces small enough for a carrier to accept.

The brush border of the small intestine is covered in them, which is why lactase deficiency is a membrane problem rather than a stomach problem.

Anchor and marker

What holds shape and what marks the cell as yours

Anchoring proteins tie the membrane to the internal skeleton and to the surrounding tissue, which is how a cell holds a shape.

Identity markers are the sugar carrying proteins and lipids on the outer face. The immune system reads them continuously, and transplant rejection is that reading returning negative.

Membrane fluidity

What sets how fluid a membrane is

Fluidity depends on how well the fatty acid tails pack together. Long, straight, saturated tails stack neatly and stiffen the membrane. Short tails and unsaturated tails with a kink in them cannot stack, so the membrane stays loose.

Temperature acts on the same packing. Warm the membrane and every part of it moves more.

11

Membrane fluidity

Both extremes of fluidity fail

The body spends real effort holding fluidity in the middle of its range.

Too stiff

Why a stiff membrane cannot signal

Proteins in a stiff membrane cannot move sideways. A receptor that cannot move cannot meet the protein it has to signal to, so the message stops at the membrane.

Signaling is not the only casualty. A stiff red cell cannot fold through a capillary, which is how a membrane change becomes an anemia.

Too loose

Why a loose membrane cannot hold a voltage

A membrane that is too loose leaks. A leaking membrane cannot hold an ion gradient, and a membrane that cannot hold a gradient cannot hold a voltage.

Week 4 is the full account of membrane voltage. All of it depends on the gradients holding.

Spur cell anemia in advanced liver disease

In advanced liver disease cholesterol accumulates in red cell membranes and stiffens them. Those cells can no longer fold through a capillary.

The spleen removes membrane from their edges, the cells become spurred, and the result is an anemia that iron does not correct.

On the course list 2. Determinants of permeability

15

Permeability

Three questions, asked in this order

Answer these three about any molecule and you have predicted whether it needs a protein.

1

Does it carry a charge?

A charged particle in water is not a bare particle. It holds a shell of water molecules around itself, and to enter the oily core it would have to strip that shell off and then sit in a nonpolar environment.

The energy cost of that is enormous. A charge is close to an absolute veto, which is why sodium sits at the bottom of the ladder despite being small.

2

Does it dissolve in fat?

Lipid solubility is the positive test. A molecule that is stable in oil treats the oily core as a road rather than a barrier. Steroid hormones are large and they cross freely, because they are lipid soluble.

This is why testosterone and cortisol bind receptors inside the cell, while insulin, a peptide, binds a receptor on the surface and signals through it.

3

How large is it?

Size rules molecules out at the top end. Anything much larger than glucose does not pass between the tails whatever else is true of it, and a protein or a polysaccharide has no chance.

Below that, size separates molecules that have already passed the first two tests. Oxygen and water are both small, and water crosses about a hundred times more slowly.

Charge first, then lipid solubility, then size.

18

Permeability in the clinic

Four decisions this ranking already makes

Anesthetics

What sets potency and what sets speed

Potency tracks lipid solubility. The more fat soluble the agent, the lower the dose that produces anesthesia. That relationship was described in 1899 and it still holds.

Speed is a separate property. How fast an agent induces is set by its blood to gas partition coefficient, and the most fat soluble agents are the slowest to induce.

Hormones

Why a steroid is slow and insulin is fast

A steroid crosses the membrane and binds a receptor in the nucleus, so it acts by changing which genes are read. That takes hours, and the effect then persists for hours.

Insulin cannot cross. It binds a receptor on the surface and acts within minutes rather than seconds. Same body, opposite speeds, and the reason is lipid solubility.

Aquaporins

Where the body installs water channels

Water crosses the bare lipid slowly. Where the body needs it fast, it installs water channels.

Antidiuretic hormone works by inserting aquaporins into the collecting duct membrane. When that fails, the patient produces liters of dilute urine a day. Week 13 is the full account.

The sodium gradient

Why plasma sodium does not leak into cells

Sodium is at 145 mM outside and 12 mM inside. A gradient that steep would collapse in seconds if the membrane passed sodium.

It does not, because sodium is charged. The gradient survives because of the veto, and every electrical event in Week 4 depends on it surviving.

Fick's law

Fick's law gives the rate of diffusion

rate is proportional to (area x concentration difference x permeability) / thickness

Permeability bundles charge, lipid solubility, size and membrane temperature into one term.

Every term on the top raises the rate as it rises. The one term on the bottom lowers the rate as it rises.

Three of the four terms can be changed by the body, and three of the four can be changed by disease. That is why this equation is used so often in the clinic.

Top makes it faster. Bottom makes it slower.

On the course list 3. Simple diffusion and Fick's law

20

Fick's law

Change one variable, predict the rate

Answer each one before you open it.

1

Double the concentration difference

The rate doubles. The difference sits on the top of the fraction and the relationship is linear, which is why a steep gradient is worth so much.

In the lung, supplemental oxygen does exactly this. It cannot change alveolar area or barrier thickness, so it changes the one variable it can reach.

2

Double the surface area

The rate doubles. Area sits on the top of the fraction as well, and the relationship is linear.

The body has invested heavily here: about 70 square meters of alveolar surface in a lung you can hold in two hands, and a small intestine folded into roughly 200 square meters. Area is obtained by folding and it pays back in proportion.

3

Double the membrane thickness

The rate halves. Thickness is the one term on the bottom of the fraction.

The alveolar barrier is about 0.5 micrometers thick. Pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary edema both add material to that barrier, and both patients report breathlessness on exertion before breathlessness at rest.

4

Double the open distance to be crossed

The time does not double. It rises four times, because diffusion time rises with the square of the distance.

The two rows ask different questions. Row three asks for the steady rate across a fixed barrier with a gradient held across it, and that rate is proportional to one over the thickness. This row asks how long one molecule takes to cover open ground with no gradient held in place, and that time rises with the square.

Three of these are linear. The fourth is a square.

Fick's law

Diffusion time rises with the square of distance

Ten times the distance is a hundred times the wait. These three numbers make that permanent.

  • 100 micrometers takes about 5 seconds. That is about as far as any cell in the body sits from a capillary.
  • 1 millimeter takes about 8 minutes.
  • 1 centimeter takes about 14 hours.

Fick's law

Bulk flow carries everything beyond a few micrometers

Diffusion is the transport system for the last few micrometers. Everything beyond that is handled by bulk flow: blood in vessels, air in airways, filtrate in tubules. The circulation exists because diffusion does not scale.

The same term sets the size of a cell, produces the necrotic center of a tumor that outgrows its blood supply, and leaves edematous tissue poorly oxygenated even when the arriving blood is fully saturated.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the diffusion of water

Osmosis is the net movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane, from where water is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated. Water is less concentrated wherever solute is more concentrated, so water moves toward solute.

Nothing new is happening here. This is diffusion with the water as the subject instead of the solute.

Osmosis

There is no water pump

The membrane has to be selective for osmosis to happen. If both the water and the solute could cross, the solute would even itself out and the water would stay where it was. The solute being trapped is what forces the water to move instead.

No water is pumped anywhere in the body. Every liter that moves between compartments in this course moves because a solute moved first.

Move the solute and the water follows.

Osmolarity

Osmolarity counts dissolved particles per liter

osmolarity (mOsm/L) = concentration (mmol/L) x particles per molecule

NaCl gives 2 particles, Na+ and Cl-. Glucose gives 1. CaCl2 gives 3.

Osmolarity is a count of dissolved particles per liter of solution. It does not depend on what the particles are, how large they are, or whether they carry a charge. One sodium ion counts the same as one glucose molecule.

Osmolality is the same count per kilogram of water rather than per liter of solution. In the body the two values are within about one percent of each other.

Salt counts twice. Sugar counts once.

Osmolarity

Plasma osmolarity is about 290 mOsm/L

Plasma is about 290 mOsm/L, and the laboratory reports it as 275 to 295 mOsm/kg. Every fluid hanging in a hospital is compared against that number, and the comparison is the first step in every question in this deck.

Work it with me

Where 308 mOsm/L comes from

Work it with me3 min

The bag

  • Sodium chloride0.9 percent
  • Molecular weight58.4 g/mol
  • Particles per molecule2
  • Plasmaabout 290 mOsm/L
  1. Turn the percentage into grams per liter. A percentage on a fluid bag means grams per 100 mL, so 0.9 percent is 0.9 g in 100 mL, which is 9 g in a liter.
  2. Turn grams into moles. 9 divided by 58.4 gives 0.154 mol/L, which is 154 mmol/L. That is the sodium concentration of the bag.
  3. Count particles. Sodium chloride separates in water into Na+ and Cl-, so each molecule gives two particles.
  4. Multiply. 154 mmol/L times 2 gives 308 mOsm/L.
  5. Compare with the patient. Plasma is about 290, so the bag is within about 6 percent, and a cell sitting in it neither swells nor shrinks measurably.

308 mOsm/L, derived rather than looked up, and close enough to plasma to be called isotonic.

Normal saline

Normal saline carries more chloride than plasma does

Along with 154 mEq/L of sodium, a liter of 0.9 percent saline delivers 154 mEq/L of chloride. Normal plasma chloride is about 100 mEq/L.

On the course list 4. Osmolarity and tonicity

35

Tonicity

Tonicity counts only the solute that stays outside

Osmolarity counts everything dissolved. Tonicity counts only the particles that cannot cross the membrane, because only those hold water on their own side.

Urea

Urea crosses, so it adds nothing to tonicity

Urea crosses membranes freely. It raises osmolarity and leaves tonicity unchanged, because it does not stay on one side.

A urea solution can match saline on osmolarity and still let a cell swell and burst. The urea walks in, evens itself out across the membrane, and the water it was supposed to hold outside comes in with it.

Mannitol

Mannitol stays outside, so every particle counts

Mannitol is a sugar alcohol that cells cannot take up. Every particle of it remains in the extracellular fluid and every particle of it counts toward tonicity.

That is why it is given intravenously to pull water out of a swollen brain. Same idea as urea, opposite result, and the only difference is whether the membrane lets the solute in.

Tonicity

Effective osmolarity is tonicity written as numbers

effective osmolarity = 2 x Na+ (mmol/L) + glucose (mg/dL) / 18

Sodium in mmol/L, glucose in mg/dL. The 18 converts glucose from mg/dL into mmol/L. Urea is left out.

Urea is left out because it crosses membranes freely and therefore contributes nothing to tonicity. Everything that remains in the formula is a solute that stays outside the cell.

The two halves arrive in different units, so check the units before you calculate. Everywhere else in this deck the work is done in mmol/L.

Osmosis and cell volume

How far a red cell can swell before it tears

A red cell carries about 136 square micrometers of membrane around about 90 femtoliters of contents. Rounded into a sphere, that same membrane would enclose about 150 femtoliters.

So a red cell can swell by roughly two thirds before the membrane has to stretch, and membranes tear rather than stretch. That surplus of surface area over volume is why the disc is biconcave, and the dimple is the spare membrane.

Shrunken cells with a spiked edge

A hypertonic solution. Water left the cell, the volume fell, and the membrane had more surface area than contents to wrap around. It buckled into spikes rather than folding smoothly.

3 percent NaCl does this in seconds. So does the plasma of a severely dehydrated patient, which is one reason severe dehydration causes confusion. Brain cells are shrinking too.

Normal

Pale centered discs, unchanged

An isotonic solution, 0.9 percent NaCl or lactated Ringer's. Water crosses constantly in both directions and the two rates are equal, so the volume does not change.

The pale center is not a hole. It is the dimple of the biconcave disc, thinner in the middle, and it is the reserve membrane that allows the cell to swell later.

Hemolysis

A clear red solution with no cells in it

A hypotonic solution. Water entered, the cell rounded into a sphere, ran out of spare membrane at about 150 femtoliters, and the membrane tore. Hemoglobin spilled out, which is why the tube is clear and red rather than cloudy.

In a graded saline series red cells begin to lyse around 0.45 percent NaCl and are gone by about 0.33 percent. Cells with a damaged spectrin mesh are already spheres, hold no spare membrane, and lyse in a stronger solution than normal cells do.

Cloudy means cells. Clear and red means the cells are gone.

The graded saline series measures an endpoint

Percentage hemolysis read at a fixed time is an endpoint rather than a rate. It measures osmotic fragility, which is set by how much spare surface area a cell holds for its volume.

The dialysis tubing bag is where you measure a rate instead. Label every tube with the variable you changed before you read it.

Why it matters

Sterile water is never infused on its own

Sterile water has an osmolarity of zero. Every red cell it meets sits in a solution with no effective solute, so water enters until the membrane tears.

Sterile water is a diluent. You dissolve a drug in it and then give that drug in something isotonic.

An osmolarity of zero is the most hypotonic solution there is.

Three consequences of infusing sterile water

  • Free hemoglobin in the plasma, which the kidney has to filter and which damages the tubules.
  • Potassium released from every burst cell. Potassium sits at 4 mM outside and 140 mM inside, so a small addition outside is a large proportional change.
  • A plasma sodium falling fast enough to swell brain cells inside a rigid skull.

Rate of correction

Brain cells adapt to a slowly abnormal osmolarity

When plasma sodium falls over days, brain cells shed internal osmoles, potassium and small organic solutes, until their volume returns to normal at the new lower outside osmolarity.

They have adapted, and they now depend on that low number. The same adaptation runs in reverse in a patient who has been hypernatremic for days, whose brain cells have gained osmoles to hold their volume.

On the course list 5. Osmosis and cell volume

49

Rate of correction

Why sodium is corrected slowly in both directions

Raise a chronically low sodium quickly and the outside osmolarity climbs while the inside is still low. Water leaves brain cells, they shrink, and osmotic demyelination can follow. It is often permanent.

Lower a chronically high sodium quickly and water floods into brain cells that are still carrying extra osmoles. That is cerebral edema inside a rigid skull.

No more than 8 mEq/L of sodium change in 24 hours, in either direction.

Where a liter of fluid lands

A 70 kg adult holds 42 L of water: 28 L inside cells and 14 L outside, of which 11 L is interstitial and 3 L is plasma.

1

One liter of 0.9 percent sodium chloride

All of it stays outside the cells, because the sodium cannot enter and the water has no reason to follow it in. It spreads through the 14 L extracellular space in the usual three to one ratio.

About 250 mL of your liter reaches plasma and about 750 mL reaches the interstitium. That is the number that matters when somebody is bleeding, because only the plasma share holds up the blood pressure.

2

One liter of D5W

The dextrose is burned within minutes, so this is effectively a liter of pure water with nothing to hold it anywhere. It distributes through all 42 L of total body water in proportion.

About 667 mL goes inside cells. The remaining third spreads through the extracellular space, and only about 83 mL ever reaches plasma, which is why D5W does little for a patient short of circulating volume.

3

One liter of 3 percent sodium chloride

All of it stays outside, and it also pulls water out of the cells. The extracellular space therefore grows by more than the liter infused.

That is the purpose of it. It is the fluid used when the problem is a swollen brain cell, and it is the fluid that harms a patient fastest if the rate is wrong.

Memory hook

Water follows the solute that cannot move

Water is not choosing, sensing, or being transported anywhere. It moves down its own gradient and it stops when the two sides match.

So the question to answer about any solution is which solute cannot cross the membrane. Find that particle and the direction of the water is already settled. A memorized list of which bag goes where fails as soon as the patient is not a healthy adult with a normal sodium.
