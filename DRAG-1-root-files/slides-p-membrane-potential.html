What this deck covers

Six things to be able to do by the end

1

The four ion concentrations

Four ions, four concentration differences, and a set of values used for the rest of the course. Potassium is high inside the cell, sodium and chloride are high outside, and free calcium is held extremely low.

2

The two forces on an ion

Concentration pushes an ion one way and membrane voltage pushes it another. The two are separate forces, and they are added to give the electrochemical gradient.

3

The Nernst equation and the equilibrium potential

The Nernst equation gives the one membrane voltage at which a given ion would stop moving. The sign of that voltage states which way the ion moves at any other membrane potential.

4

Why the resting potential is negative

At rest the membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to anything else, so the cell settles near the potassium equilibrium potential. Change the permeability and the resting potential moves.

5

What opens an ion channel

Four answers: nothing, a change in voltage, a chemical, or a physical force. Each of the four has a place in the body where it accounts for the whole behavior.

6

The four terms for a change in voltage

Depolarization, repolarization, hyperpolarization and overshoot. Four terms, one tracing, and you label them by hand this week.

Calcium

Free calcium is held about ten thousand times lower inside

Free calcium inside a resting cell is 0.0001 mmol/L, against 1.2 mmol/L outside. Potassium differs across the membrane by a factor of 35 and sodium by 12. The cell spends energy continuously to hold calcium at this ratio.

What the low internal calcium is for

Because the resting concentration is so low, opening a calcium channel for a single millisecond raises free calcium inside ten fold or more. That rise fuses a vesicle at a synapse, starts a muscle contraction, and makes a gland secrete.

10

Two forces

Two forces act on every ion at the membrane

Force one

The concentration difference

The concentration difference pushes an ion from where it is crowded toward where it is not. This is the chemical driving force, and it is independent of the charge on the ion. With 140 mmol/L potassium inside and 4 mmol/L outside, potassium is pushed out.

Force two

The membrane voltage

The interior of a resting cell is negative, and negative charge attracts positive charge. The voltage pulls positive ions in and pushes negative ions out. This is the electrical driving force, and it is independent of concentration.

The sum of the two forces is the electrochemical gradient. Net movement follows the sum.

On the course list 15. Ion distribution and electrochemical gradients

11

Ion by ion

At -70 mV, which way is each ion pushed?

K+

The two forces nearly cancel

The chemical force pushes potassium out, because there is 35 times more inside. The electrical force pulls it back in, because the interior is negative and potassium is positive. These nearly balance, so the outward leak is small but not zero, and a pump is required to replace what leaks.

Na+

Both forces point inward

The chemical force pushes sodium in, because there is 12 times more outside. The electrical force also pulls it in, because sodium is positive and the interior is negative. Nothing opposes it. At rest almost no sodium channels are open, and that is the only reason sodium does not flood in.

Cl-

The forces oppose each other

The chemical force pushes chloride in, since there is 10 times more outside. The electrical force pushes it back out, because a negative ion is repelled by a negative interior. In many cells these come close to balancing at rest, so chloride tends to stabilize the membrane rather than move it.

Ca2+

The largest driving force on any ion

The chemical force pushes calcium in by a factor of about 10,000. The electrical force also pulls it in, and calcium carries two positive charges, so it responds to the voltage twice as strongly. Opening a calcium channel produces a large inward flux, which is what the cell uses calcium for.

Sodium and calcium are the two ions where both forces point the same way.

12

The equation

The Nernst equation gives the equilibrium potential for one ion

The equilibrium potential is the membrane voltage at which the electrical force on an ion exactly cancels the chemical force.

E = (61 / z) x log (outside / inside)

E is in mV. The 61 bundles the physical constants at 37 C. z is the ion's charge: +1 for K+ and Na+, -1 for Cl-, +2 for Ca2+. The log is base 10.

Put outside on top, always. Keep the sign of the answer, because the sign states which way the ion moves.

Check the ratio before using the calculator. If outside is smaller than inside, the log is negative and the answer comes out negative.

On the course list 16. Nernst equation

13

The equation

The equation describes one ion in isolation

The Nernst equation answers a question about a single ion with no other ion present. No real cell is permeable to only one ion, so no real cell sits exactly at any equilibrium potential.

The distance between the Nernst prediction and the measured resting potential is used later in this deck to measure sodium permeability.

14

Worked example

Calculate the potassium equilibrium potential at normal concentrations

Work it with me3 min

Potassium at rest

  • Inside the cell140 mmol/L
  • Outside the cell4 mmol/L
  • Chargeplus one
  • Temperature37 C
  1. Write the equation. E K = (61 / z) x log (K outside / K inside).
  2. Put in z. Potassium carries one positive charge, so 61 divided by 1 is 61. E K = 61 x log (4 / 140).
  3. Take the ratio. 4 divided by 140 is 0.0286. The ratio is less than 1, so the log is negative and the answer is negative.
  4. Take the log. log (0.0286) = -1.54.
  5. Multiply. 61 x -1.54 = -94 mV.

E K = -94 mV. This value is used for the rest of the course.

At any membrane potential less negative than -94 mV, potassium leaves the cell.

You will do this in lab You use this form of the equation, 61 over z times the log, throughout the membrane potential lab packet. Every calculation on that packet is worked by hand in pen, so bring a calculator with a log key.

20

Reading the answer

The rule for reading an equilibrium potential

If the membrane potential is more negative than a positive ion's equilibrium potential, that ion moves into the cell. If it is less negative, that ion moves out.

An ion moves in whichever direction carries the membrane toward that ion's own equilibrium potential.

21

Reading the answer

The rule applied to two ions

Potassium

Kofi at -75 mV, E K -107 mV

The membrane is less negative than E K, so potassium leaves the cell whenever a potassium channel opens. The direction is the same as in a normal cell, and the driving force is smaller.

Sodium

A normal cell at -70 mV, E Na +66 mV

The membrane is far more negative than E Na, so sodium enters the instant a sodium channel opens. The gap is 136 mV wide, against 24 mV for potassium, so the push on sodium is much larger.

Ions move toward their own equilibrium potential and carry the membrane potential with them.

22

Driving force

Driving force is the gap between two voltages

The driving force on an ion is the distance between the membrane potential and that ion's equilibrium potential. Sodium at rest has one of the largest gaps in the body, 136 mV.

An ion sitting at its own equilibrium potential has no driving force, however large the concentration difference across the membrane is.

The resting potential

A resting neuron sits at about -70 mV

On the course list 17. Resting membrane potential

24

The resting potential

Why the cell sits near the potassium equilibrium potential

Permeability

Which ion has the most open channels at rest?

Potassium. A resting membrane carries many potassium leak channels that are open continuously, and very few open sodium channels. The membrane is roughly 25 to 40 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium.

The 24 mV gap

Why the cell is not at -94 mV

Sodium permeability is small but not zero. The trickle of sodium entering brings positive charge in and holds the membrane about 24 mV less negative than potassium alone would. The resting potential is a compromise weighted by permeability.

The pump

What keeps the gradients from running down

The Na+/K+ ATPase, met earlier this week. It exports 3 Na+ and imports 2 K+ per cycle, replacing what leaks. Because it moves one net positive charge out per cycle it also adds a few mV directly. Stop the pump and the resting potential decays.

The resting potential belongs to whichever ion has the most open channels. At rest that is potassium.

On the course list 17. Resting membrane potential

25

Predict it

Change one thing and state which way the resting potential moves

1

Open more potassium channels

Potassium permeability rises, so the membrane is pulled harder toward E K at -94 mV. The cell becomes more negative, which is hyperpolarization. It is now farther from threshold and harder to fire. Many inhibitory signals act this way.

2

Double sodium permeability

The membrane is pulled toward E Na at +66 mV. The cell becomes less negative, which is depolarization, and it is now closer to threshold. Do this hard enough and fast enough and an action potential begins.

3

Raise extracellular potassium from 4 to 7 mmol/L

The potassium gradient is smaller, so E K moves from -94 mV toward -79 mV. The resting potential follows to about -64 mV. The cell depolarizes and sits closer to threshold. A concentration changed here rather than a permeability, and the direction rule still applies.

4

Block the Na+/K+ ATPase

Little happens in the first second, because the pump contributes only a few mV directly. The gradients then run down, sodium accumulates inside, potassium leaks away, and the resting potential decays toward zero over minutes. That collapse is how ischemic tissue dies once the ATP runs out.

5

Why digoxin does not act by that route

Digoxin blocks only a fraction of the pumps, so the resting potential does not collapse. The change that matters is downstream: a shallower sodium gradient means the sodium calcium exchanger moves less calcium out of the cell, so the heart contracts more forcefully.

The membrane moves toward the equilibrium potential of whichever ion just became more permeable.

Hyperkalemia

Sustained depolarization inactivates sodium channels

Voltage gated sodium channels open in response to depolarization, and they also inactivate if the membrane stays depolarized. A cell held at -60 mV or above has a growing fraction of its sodium channels stuck in the inactivated state, and an inactivated channel cannot open at any stimulus strength.

Hyperkalemia

Easier to fire, then harder, then unable to fire

At K+ 6 mmol/L a cardiac cell sits near -65 mV, closer to threshold and easier to excite. Above that, inactivation takes over.

Early hyperkalemia gives tall, narrow peaked T waves, because repolarization is fast. Higher still, the P wave flattens and disappears, the QRS widens as conduction slows, and the tracing merges into a sine wave.

Why a potassium infusion is given slowly

Back to Kofi

Hypokalemia in cardiac muscle runs the other way

The potassium currents that repolarize a cardiac cell depend on extracellular potassium. When potassium outside falls, those currents become slower. Repolarization drags out, and the tracing shows a flattened T wave, a U wave after it, and a long QT interval.

A cell that repolarizes slowly and unevenly can be re excited by a stray beat, which is the irregular rhythm on Kofi's monitor. His potassium is corrected before the rhythm worsens.

The pump

The Na+/K+ ATPase maintains both gradients

Each cycle moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in and spends one ATP. That holds sodium low inside and potassium high inside against the leaks.

Around 20 to 25 percent of resting energy expenditure goes to this one job, and the share is far higher in a working neuron.

36

The pump

The pump builds the gradients, the channels make the voltage

Three positive charges leave for every two that enter, so the pump is slightly electrogenic and contributes a few mV of the resting negativity directly. Only a few, so do not overstate it in an exam answer.

The pump does not create the membrane potential. It builds the gradients, and open potassium channels convert those gradients into a voltage.

37

Ion channels

Four kinds of gate

Channels are classified by what has to happen before the pore lets ions through.

Leak

Nothing opens it

A leak channel has no gate, so it is open continuously and ions pass whenever a driving force exists. The potassium leak channel is the reason a resting potential exists. Found in every cell, and dominant at rest.

Voltage gated

A change in membrane voltage

The protein carries charged segments that move when the electric field across the membrane changes, and that movement swings the gate. Depolarization is the usual trigger. Found in axons, skeletal muscle, and every cell of the heart.

Ligand gated

A chemical binds to it

The channel is also a receptor, so the molecule that opens it is the message. The ligand can be a neurotransmitter arriving from outside or a second messenger arriving from inside. Found at the neuromuscular junction and at chemical synapses in the brain.

Mechanically gated

Physical force on the membrane

Stretch, pressure, bending or vibration deforms the membrane and pulls the pore open, converting a mechanical event directly into an electrical one. Found in hair cells of the inner ear, in baroreceptors, and in touch receptors in skin.

Four gates, four triggers: nothing, voltage, a chemical, a push.

39

Where each one is met

One location in the body for each channel type

1

Leak channels in a resting neuron

Potassium leak channels are open every second, and the steady outward trickle of potassium is what makes the interior negative. They also make the resting potential track extracellular potassium closely, which is the mechanism behind Kofi's presentation.

2

Voltage gated sodium channels and local anesthetic

Lidocaine and the other local anesthetics enter voltage gated sodium channels and plug them, so the nerve under the injection cannot depolarize to threshold however hard the drill works. The signal never starts. Nothing about the tooth has changed.

3

Ligand gated channels at the neuromuscular junction

Acetylcholine released at the neuromuscular junction binds the nicotinic receptor on the muscle fiber, which is a ligand gated channel. It opens, sodium enters, and the muscle depolarizes. Curare blocks that receptor, producing paralysis without loss of consciousness.

4

Mechanically gated channels in the inner ear

A sound wave bends the stereocilia of a hair cell in the cochlea, and the bending pulls the channels open directly. Ions enter and the cell depolarizes. A mechanical event is converted straight into voltage. Week 7 begins here.

The vocabulary

Four terms for a change in membrane potential

Each term names a direction of movement from where the membrane started rather than a fixed voltage.

Depolarization

Toward zero

Less negative than rest

The membrane potential becomes less negative, moving from -70 mV toward 0 mV. It is usually positive charge entering the cell, most often sodium or calcium. A depolarized cell is closer to threshold and easier to fire.

Repolarization

Back to rest

Returning toward the resting potential

The membrane returns toward the resting potential after having been depolarized. It is usually potassium leaving the cell and carrying positive charge out. The term describes a return, so a cell that was never depolarized cannot repolarize.

Hyperpolarization

Past rest

More negative than the resting potential

The membrane potential becomes more negative than the resting potential, for example -80 mV when rest is -70 mV. Potassium leaving or chloride entering produces it. A hyperpolarized cell is farther from threshold and harder to fire. Kofi's cells are in this state.

Overshoot

Above 0 mV

Interior briefly positive

The part of a depolarization that carries the interior above 0 mV. During an action potential the peak reaches about +30 mV. Overshoot is part of depolarization rather than a separate phase that follows it.

Repolarization returns to rest. Hyperpolarization goes past it.
